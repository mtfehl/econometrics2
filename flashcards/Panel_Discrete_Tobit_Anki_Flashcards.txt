What is panel data and what are its two dimensions?	A collection of \(N\) time series where the same individual units are followed over \(T\) time periods.<br><br><b>Two dimensions:</b><br>1. Cross-sectional: \(N\) units, indexed by \(i = 1,\ldots,N\)<br>2. Time-series: \(T\) periods, indexed by \(t = 1,\ldots,T\)<br><br>Observable info: \(y\) is \(NT \times 1\), \(X\) is \(NT \times K\). For each unit \(i\): \(y_i\) is \(T \times 1\), \(X_i\) is \(T \times K\).	PanelData::Basics
What is the difference between exogenous and endogenous attrition in panel data?	<b>Exogenous attrition:</b> The process of losing units is independent of the dependent variable. Balanced and unbalanced panels share the same properties. OLS remains valid.<br><br><b>Endogenous attrition:</b> Attrition is related to the outcome (e.g., firms exit because of negative profits). Over time, the sample becomes less representative. This selection process must be modeled.<br><br>The course assumes exogenous attrition and \(T_i = T\) for all \(i\).	PanelData::Basics
Why do we need panel data? Give two key reasons.	1. <b>Endogeneity from unobserved heterogeneity:</b> If an important variable \(f_i\) is unobservable and correlated with regressors, OLS is inconsistent. Panel data lets you eliminate time-invariant \(f_i\) by first-differencing or within-transformation, without needing an IV.<br><br>2. <b>Dynamics:</b> Many economic decisions are dynamic — past outcomes affect current ones (habit formation, human capital). Panel data is required to estimate dynamic models with individual heterogeneity.<br><br>Cross-sectional solutions (IV) may not be feasible; panel data offers alternatives.	PanelData::Basics
Write the basic panel data model and define each component.	\(y_{it} = x'_{it}\beta + f_i + u_{it}\)<br><br>• \(v_{it} = f_i + u_{it}\): composite error<br>• \(f_i\): unobserved effect (time-invariant, no \(t\) subscript)<br>• \(u_{it}\): idiosyncratic time-varying shock<br>• \(x_{it}\): \(K \times 1\) vector of regressors (may or may not vary over time)<br>• \(\beta\): \(K \times 1\) parameter vector<br><br>The key question: how is \(f_i\) related to \(x_{it}\)? This determines which estimator to use.	PanelData::Model
What is the difference between strict and weak exogeneity?	<b>Strict exogeneity:</b><br>\(E(u_{it}|X_i, f_i) = 0\)<br><br>The error at time \(t\) is uncorrelated with regressors at ALL times (past, present, future). Implies \(E(x_{it}u_{is}) = 0\) for all \(t,s\).<br><br><b>Weak exogeneity (predetermined):</b><br>\(E(u_{it}|\{x_{is}\}_{s \leq t}, f_i) = 0\)<br><br>The error at time \(t\) is uncorrelated with regressors up to and including time \(t\), but may be correlated with future regressors.<br><br>Strict exogeneity rules out feedback from \(u_{it}\) to future \(x\). Violated in dynamic models where \(x_{it} = y_{i,t-1}\).	PanelData::Assumptions
What is the difference between Random Effects and Fixed Effects assumptions?	<b>Random Effects:</b><br>\(E(f_i|X_i) = E(f_i) = 0\)<br>Implication: \(\text{corr}(x_{it}, f_i) = 0\)<br>The unobserved effect is uncorrelated with regressors.<br><br><b>Fixed Effects:</b><br>\(E(f_i|X_i) = g(X_i) \neq 0\)<br>Implication: \(\text{corr}(x_{it}, f_i) \neq 0\) generally<br>The unobserved effect IS correlated with regressors.<br><br>RE is more efficient but requires a stronger assumption. FE is more robust but cannot identify effects of time-invariant variables.	PanelData::Assumptions
State the four Random Effects assumptions (RE1-RE4).	<b>RE1:</b> Random individual effects: \(E(f_i|X_i) = 0\)<br><br><b>RE2:</b> Strict exogeneity: \(E(u_{it}|X_i, f_i) = 0\)<br><br><b>RE3:</b> Homoskedasticity:<br>\(\text{Var}(f_i|X_i) = \sigma_f^2\)<br>\(\text{Var}(u_{it}|X_i, f_i) = \sigma_u^2\)<br>\(\text{Cov}(u_{it}, u_{is}|X_i, f_i) = 0\) for \(t \neq s\)<br><br><b>RE4:</b> Linearly independent regressors: \(E(X_i'X_i) = M_{XX}\) has rank \(K\).<br><br>RE1+RE2+RE3 imply: \(\text{Cov}(v_{it}, v_{is}|X_i) = \sigma_f^2 + \delta_{ts}\sigma_u^2\)	PanelData::RE
Why is Pooled OLS consistent but inefficient under the RE assumptions?	<b>Consistent:</b> Under RE1+RE2, \(E(v_i|X_i) = 0\), so:<br>\(\text{plim } \hat{\beta}^{POLS} = \beta + M_{XX}^{-1} \cdot 0 = \beta\)<br><br>POLS only needs contemporaneous uncorrelation for consistency — even weaker than strict exogeneity.<br><br><b>Inefficient:</b> The composite errors \(v_{it} = f_i + u_{it}\) are serially correlated due to \(f_i\):<br>\(\text{Cov}(v_{it}, v_{is}|X_i) = \sigma_f^2 \neq 0\) for \(t \neq s\)<br><br>POLS ignores this serial correlation. Standard errors are wrong. The RE (GLS) estimator accounts for the error structure and is more efficient.	PanelData::RE
Write the Random Effects estimator and its covariance matrix \(V\).	\(\hat{\beta}^{RE} = \left(\sum_i X_i' V^{-1} X_i\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_i X_i' V^{-1} y_i\right)\)<br><br>where \(V = E(v_i v_i'|X_i) = \sigma_u^2 I_T + \sigma_f^2 J_T\)<br><br>\(I_T\): identity matrix, \(J_T\): \(T \times T\) matrix of ones.<br><br><b>Asymptotic distribution:</b><br>\(\sqrt{N}(\hat{\beta}^{RE} - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, E[X_i'V^{-1}X_i]^{-1})\)<br><br><b>Feasible GLS:</b> Estimate \(\sigma_u^2\) and \(\sigma_f^2\) from POLS residuals, construct \(\hat{V}\), substitute. Feasible estimator is neither linear nor unbiased but is consistent and asymptotically equivalent to infeasible RE.	PanelData::RE
State the Fixed Effects assumptions (FE1-FE4).	<b>FE1:</b> Fixed individual effects: \(E(f_i|X_i) \neq 0\)<br><br><b>FE2:</b> Strict exogeneity: \(E(u_{it}|X_i, f_i) = 0\)<br><br><b>FE3:</b> Homoskedasticity:<br>\(\text{Var}(u_{it}|X_i, f_i) = \sigma_u^2\)<br>\(\text{Cov}(u_{it}, u_{is}|X_i, f_i) = 0\) for \(t \neq s\)<br><br><b>FE4:</b> Linearly independent regressors: \(E(\tilde{X}_i'\tilde{X}_i) = M_{\tilde{X}\tilde{X}}\) has rank \(K^*\)<br><br>Note: \(\tilde{X}\) can only include time-varying regressors. Time-invariant variables are wiped out by the within transformation.	PanelData::FE
Derive the Fixed Effects (within-groups) estimator.	Define individual means: \(\bar{y}_i = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t y_{it}\), \(\bar{x}_i = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t x_{it}\)<br><br>Center: \(\tilde{y}_{it} = y_{it} - \bar{y}_i\), \(\tilde{x}_{it} = x_{it} - \bar{x}_i\)<br><br>In matrix notation: \(\tilde{y}_i = Q_T y_i\), \(\tilde{X}_i = Q_T X_i\), where \(Q_T = I_T - P_T\) and \(P_T = \frac{1}{T}\mathbf{1}_T\mathbf{1}_T'\).<br><br>Centered model: \(\tilde{y}_i = \tilde{X}_i\beta + \tilde{u}_i\) (the \(f_i\) disappears!)<br><br>\(\hat{\beta}^{FE} = \left(\sum_i \tilde{X}_i'\tilde{X}_i\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_i \tilde{X}_i'\tilde{y}_i\right) = \left(\sum_i X_i'Q_TX_i\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_i X_i'Q_Ty_i\right)\)<br><br>Time-invariant covariates vanish: \(\tilde{z}_i = z_i - z_i = 0\).	PanelData::FE
Show that the FE estimator is consistent under FE1-FE4.	\(\hat{\beta}^{FE} = \beta + \left(\frac{1}{N}\tilde{X}'\tilde{X}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{N}\tilde{X}'u\right)\)<br><br>(Note: \(\tilde{X}'\tilde{u} = \tilde{X}'u\) since \(Q_T f_i\mathbf{1}_T = 0\))<br><br>By LLN: \(\frac{1}{N}\tilde{X}'\tilde{X} \xrightarrow{p} M_{\tilde{X}\tilde{X}}\), invertible by FE4.<br><br>By FE2 (strict exogeneity): \(E(\tilde{X}_i'u_i) = 0\), so \(\frac{1}{N}\tilde{X}'u \xrightarrow{p} 0\).<br><br>\(\text{plim } \hat{\beta}^{FE} = \beta\) ✓<br><br><b>Asymptotic distribution:</b><br>\(\sqrt{N}(\hat{\beta}^{FE} - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_u^2 E[\tilde{X}_i'\tilde{X}_i]^{-1})\)<br><br>Estimate \(\sigma_u^2\) with \(\hat{\sigma}_u^2 = \frac{1}{N(T-1)-K}\sum_i\sum_t \tilde{\hat{u}}_{it}^2\). Note \(N(T-1)\) not \(NT\) because demeaning costs one degree of freedom per unit.	PanelData::FE
State the First-Difference assumptions (FD1-FD4) and the FD estimator.	<b>FD1:</b> \(E(f_i|X_i) \neq 0\)<br><b>FD2:</b> Strict exogeneity: \(E(u_{it}|X_i, f_i) = 0\)<br><b>FD3:</b> Homoskedasticity after differencing: \(\text{Var}(\Delta u_{it}|X_i, f_i) = \sigma_e^2\), \(\text{Cov}(\Delta u_{it}, \Delta u_{is}|X_i, f_i) = 0\) for \(t \neq s\)<br><b>FD4:</b> \(E(\Delta X_i'\Delta X_i)\) has rank \(K^*\)<br><br>First-difference: \(\Delta y_{it} = \Delta x_{it}'\beta + \Delta u_{it}\) (fixed effect eliminated)<br><br>\(\hat{\beta}^{FD} = \left(\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta X_i\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta y_i\right)\)<br><br>Under FD3: \(\sqrt{N}(\hat{\beta}^{FD} - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_e^2 E[\Delta X_i'\Delta X_i]^{-1})\)	PanelData::FD
When is FD more efficient than FE, and vice versa?	<b>FE more efficient:</b> When \(u_{it}\) is serially uncorrelated (iid). Then FE3 holds and FE uses the within variation optimally.<br><br><b>FD more efficient:</b> When \(u_{it}\) follows a random walk (\(u_{it} = u_{i,t-1} + e_{it}\)). Then \(\Delta u_{it} = e_{it}\) is iid, so FD3 holds.<br><br>Often the truth lies between these extremes.<br><br><b>When T = 2:</b> FE and FD produce identical estimates.<br><br><b>Diagnostic:</b> If FE and FD give very different results (beyond sampling error), worry about the strict exogeneity assumption.	PanelData::FD
Explain the cost of first-differencing.	Consider: \(y_{it} = \alpha + x_{it}\beta + u_{it}\) with iid errors, \(T = 2\).<br><br><b>OLS variance:</b> \(\text{Avar}(\hat{\beta}^{OLS}) = \frac{\sigma_u^2}{2N\text{Var}(x_{it})}\)<br><br><b>FD variance:</b> \(\text{Avar}(\hat{\beta}^{FD}) = \frac{2\sigma_u^2}{N\text{Var}(\Delta x_{it})} = \frac{2\sigma_u^2}{N[\text{Var}(x_{i1}) + \text{Var}(x_{i2}) - 2\text{Cov}(x_{i1},x_{i2})]}\)<br><br><b>Two costs:</b><br>1. <b>More noise:</b> \(2\sigma_u^2\) in numerator (differencing doubles the error variance)<br>2. <b>Less signal:</b> If \(\text{Cov}(x_{i1},x_{i2}) > 0\) (typical for economic variables), \(\text{Var}(\Delta x)\) is small<br><br>FD should only be used when justified (endogeneity from time-invariant unobservables).	PanelData::FD
How does the RE estimator relate to FE? Show the decomposition.	\(V = \sigma_u^2 I_T + \sigma_f^2 J_T = \sigma_u^2(Q_T + \eta P_T)\)<br>where \(\eta = (\sigma_u^2 + T\sigma_f^2)/\sigma_u^2\) and \(Q_T = I_T - P_T\).<br><br>\(V^{-1} = \frac{1}{\sigma_u^2}Q_T + \frac{1}{\eta}P_T\)<br><br>The RE estimator decomposes into:<br>\(\hat{\beta}^{RE} = \left(\underbrace{\sum X_i'Q_TX_i}_{\text{within}} + \frac{1}{\eta}\underbrace{\sum X_i'P_TX_i}_{\text{between}}\right)^{-1}\left(\underbrace{\sum X_i'Q_Ty_i}_{\text{within}} + \frac{1}{\eta}\underbrace{\sum X_i'P_Ty_i}_{\text{between}}\right)\)<br><br>RE uses BOTH within and between variation. FE uses only within variation. RE is at least as efficient as FE (the between component adds information), but RE requires \(E(f_i|X_i) = 0\).	PanelData::Comparison
What is the Hausman test and when do you use it?	Tests \(H_0: E(f_i|X_i) = 0\) (RE assumptions hold).<br><br>Under \(H_0\): both FE and RE consistent, RE more efficient.<br>Under \(H_1\): RE inconsistent, FE still consistent.<br><br>Test statistic:<br>\(H = (\hat{\beta}^{FE} - \hat{\beta}^{RE})'\left[\text{Avar}(\hat{\beta}^{FE}) - \text{Avar}(\hat{\beta}^{RE})\right]^{-1}(\hat{\beta}^{FE} - \hat{\beta}^{RE})\)<br><br>\(H \sim \chi^2(K)\) under \(H_0\).<br><br>If reject: use FE. If fail to reject: RE is preferred (more efficient + identifies time-invariant effects).<br><br><b>Logic:</b> Under \(H_0\), both estimate the same \(\beta\), so their difference should be small. A large difference signals RE is inconsistent.	PanelData::Comparison
Why is the FE estimator biased in dynamic panel models? (Nickell bias)	Model: \(y_{it} = \alpha y_{i,t-1} + f_i + u_{it}\)<br><br>The within transformation gives: \(y_{it} - \bar{y}_i = \alpha(y_{i,t-1} - \bar{y}_{i,-}) + (u_{it} - \bar{u}_i)\)<br><br><b>Problem:</b> \(\bar{y}_{i,-}\) contains \(y_{it}\) terms that depend on \(u_{it}\), so \(y_{i,t-1} - \bar{y}_{i,-}\) is correlated with \(u_{it} - \bar{u}_i\).<br><br>Specifically: \(E(u_{it}(y_{i,t-1} - \bar{y}_{i,-})) = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=t}^{T-1}\alpha^{s-t}\sigma_u^2 < 0\)<br><br>The FE estimator of \(\alpha\) is <b>downward biased</b>.<br><br>Bias vanishes as \(T \to \infty\) (if \(|\alpha| < 1\)), but NOT as \(N \to \infty\). Useless in typical micro panels where \(T\) is small.	PanelData::Dynamic
Why does FD also fail in dynamic panel models, and what is the solution?	First-difference: \(\Delta y_{it} = \alpha\Delta y_{i,t-1} + \Delta u_{it}\)<br><br>\(\Delta y_{i,t-1} = y_{i,t-1} - y_{i,t-2}\) and \(\Delta u_{it} = u_{it} - u_{it-1}\).<br><br><b>Problem:</b> \(y_{i,t-1}\) depends on \(u_{i,t-1}\), which appears in \(\Delta u_{it}\). So \(\text{Cov}(\Delta y_{i,t-1}, \Delta u_{it}) \neq 0\).<br><br><b>Solution: Anderson-Hsiao estimator.</b> Use \(y_{i,t-2}\) as instrument for \(\Delta y_{i,t-1}\).<br><br><b>Validity:</b><br>• Relevant: \(y_{i,t-2}\) correlated with \(\Delta y_{i,t-1}\) (appears directly and through AR structure)<br>• Exogenous: \(y_{i,t-2}\) depends on \(u_{i,t-2}\) and earlier; \(\Delta u_{it}\) involves only \(u_{it}, u_{i,t-1}\). No serial correlation → \(\text{Cov}(y_{i,t-2}, \Delta u_{it}) = 0\).	PanelData::Dynamic
Explain the Arellano-Bond estimator and its instrument matrix.	AB exploits ALL valid moment conditions, not just one per period (like Anderson-Hsiao).<br><br>For the FD equation at time \(t\), any \(y_{i,s}\) with \(s \leq t-2\) is valid:<br><br>At \(t=2\): instrument \(y_{i0}\) (1 instrument)<br>At \(t=3\): instruments \(y_{i0}, y_{i1}\) (2 instruments)<br>At \(t=4\): instruments \(y_{i0}, y_{i1}, y_{i2}\) (3 instruments)<br><br>Instrument matrix is block-diagonal:<br>\(Z_i = \text{diag}(y_{i1}, (y_{i1},y_{i2}), \ldots, (y_{i1},\ldots,y_{i,T-2}))\)<br><br>Moment conditions: \(E(Z_i'\Delta u_i) = 0\)<br><br><b>One-step GMM:</b> uses \(H\) matrix (tridiagonal: 2 on diagonal, -1 off-diagonal) as weighting.<br><b>Two-step GMM:</b> uses \(\hat{W} = (\frac{1}{N}\sum Z_i'\widehat{\Delta u}_i\widehat{\Delta u}_i'Z_i)^{-1}\). More efficient, robust to heteroskedasticity.	PanelData::Dynamic
What is System GMM and when is it preferred over Difference GMM?	<b>Problem with Difference GMM:</b> When \(\alpha \approx 1\) (highly persistent series), past levels are poor predictors of current differences → weak instruments → biased and imprecise estimates.<br><br><b>System GMM</b> (Arellano-Bover/Blundell-Bond) adds the equation in levels:<br>\(y_{it} = \alpha y_{i,t-1} + f_i + u_{it}\)<br><br>Uses <b>lagged differences</b> as instruments for the levels equation, assuming \(E(\Delta y_{i,t-1} \cdot f_i) = 0\).<br><br>Combines:<br>• Differenced equation: lagged levels as instruments<br>• Levels equation: lagged differences as instruments<br><br>Massive efficiency gains for persistent data. But the additional assumption (\(\Delta y\) uncorrelated with \(f_i\)) is substantive and must be justified.	PanelData::Dynamic
What are the three problems with the Linear Probability Model (LPM)?	1. <b>Non-normal disturbances:</b> \(u_i\) takes only two values: \(1 - x_i'\beta\) with prob \(x_i'\beta\), and \(-x_i'\beta\) with prob \(1 - x_i'\beta\).<br><br>2. <b>Heteroskedasticity:</b> \(\text{Var}(u_i|x_i) = (x_i'\beta)(1 - x_i'\beta) = \Pr(y_i=1|x_i)\Pr(y_i=0|x_i)\). Variance depends on \(x_i\).<br><br>3. <b>Unbounded probabilities:</b> \(E(y_i|x_i) = x_i'\beta\) can be outside \([0,1]\). Predicted probabilities can be negative or greater than 1.<br><br><b>Advantage of LPM:</b> Marginal effects are simply \(\beta_j\) — constant and easy to interpret. Often used as a quick check.	DiscreteChoice::LPM
What are the Logit and Probit models? How do they solve the LPM problems?	Both constrain probabilities to \([0,1]\) using a CDF as the link function:<br><br><b>Logit:</b> \(\Pr(y_i=1|x_i) = \Lambda(x_i'\beta) = \frac{e^{x_i'\beta}}{1+e^{x_i'\beta}}\)<br><br><b>Probit:</b> \(\Pr(y_i=1|x_i) = \Phi(x_i'\beta)\)<br><br>Both are monotone increasing, mapping \((-\infty, +\infty) \to (0,1)\).<br><br>The logistic has slightly thicker tails than the normal. In practice, results are very similar.<br><br><b>Cost:</b> Nonlinear in \(\beta\) → cannot use OLS. Must use MLE. Coefficients are not directly interpretable as marginal effects.	DiscreteChoice::Binary
Derive the log-likelihood function for a binary choice model.	For individual \(i\):<br>• If \(y_i = 1\): \(L^i(\beta) = F(x_i'\beta)\)<br>• If \(y_i = 0\): \(L^i(\beta) = 1 - F(x_i'\beta)\)<br><br>Compactly: \(L^i = F(x_i'\beta)^{y_i}(1-F(x_i'\beta))^{1-y_i}\)<br><br>Under random sampling, joint likelihood:<br>\(L(\beta) = \prod_{i=1}^N F(x_i'\beta)^{y_i}(1-F(x_i'\beta))^{1-y_i}\)<br><br>Log-likelihood:<br>\(\ell(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^N [y_i \ln F(x_i'\beta) + (1-y_i)\ln(1-F(x_i'\beta))]\)<br><br>For Logit: \(F = \Lambda\). For Probit: \(F = \Phi\).	DiscreteChoice::MLE
What are the FOCs for the binary choice MLE? How do they simplify for Logit?	<b>General FOC:</b><br>\(\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \beta} = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{y_i - F(x_i'\beta)}{F(x_i'\beta)(1-F(x_i'\beta))} F'(x_i'\beta) x_i = 0\)<br><br><b>Logit simplification:</b> Since \(\Lambda'(z) = \Lambda(z)(1-\Lambda(z))\), the fraction cancels:<br>\(\sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \Lambda(x_i'\beta))x_i = 0\)<br><br>Beautifully simple — resembles OLS normal equations with \(\Lambda(x_i'\beta)\) replacing \(x_i'\beta\).<br><br><b>Probit:</b> Does not simplify as cleanly:<br>\(\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{\phi(x_i'\beta)}{\Phi(x_i'\beta)(1-\Phi(x_i'\beta))}(y_i - \Phi(x_i'\beta))x_i = 0\)<br>Weight \(w_i\) varies across observations.	DiscreteChoice::MLE
How do you compute and interpret marginal effects in binary choice models?	<b>General:</b><br>\(\frac{\partial \Pr(y_i=1|x_i)}{\partial x_{ij}} = F'(x_i'\beta)\beta_j\)<br><br>The marginal effect depends on the evaluation point \(x_i\) (unlike LPM where it's constant \(\beta_j\)).<br><br><b>Logit:</b> \(F' = p_i(1-p_i)\beta_j\), maximum at \(p = 0.5\)<br><br><b>Probit:</b> \(F' = \phi(x_i'\beta)\beta_j\)<br><br><b>Reporting conventions:</b><br>1. Marginal effects at the mean (MEM): evaluate at \(\bar{x}\)<br>2. Average marginal effects (AME): \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_i F'(x_i'\hat{\beta})\hat{\beta}_j\)<br><br><b>Amemiya's rule of thumb:</b> \(1.6\hat{\beta}^{Probit} \approx \hat{\beta}^{Logit}\), \(4\hat{\beta}^{LPM} \approx \hat{\beta}^{Logit}\)	DiscreteChoice::MarginalEffects
What is the Information Matrix Equality and why does it matter?	If the model is correctly specified: \(B_0 = -A_0\), where:<br><br>\(A_0 = E\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ln L_i}{\partial\beta\partial\beta'}\bigg|\beta_0\right]\) (expected Hessian)<br><br>\(B_0 = E\left[\frac{\partial \ln L_i}{\partial\beta}\frac{\partial \ln L_i}{\partial\beta'}\bigg|\beta_0\right]\) (outer product of gradients)<br><br><b>Consequence:</b> Three equivalent variance estimators:<br>1. OPG: \(\hat{V} = \hat{B}_0^{-1}\) (only first derivatives needed)<br>2. Hessian: \(\tilde{V} = -\hat{A}_0^{-1}\)<br>3. Expected Hessian: evaluated at \(\hat{\beta}\)<br><br><b>If misspecified:</b> \(B_0 \neq -A_0\), use sandwich: \(V = A_0^{-1}B_0 A_0^{-1'}\)	DiscreteChoice::Inference
What is the Multinomial Logit (MNL) model? What does it assume?	\(\Pr(y_i = j|x_i) = \frac{e^{x_i'\beta_j}}{\sum_{l=1}^m e^{x_i'\beta_l}}\)<br><br>Normalize \(\beta_1 = 0\) for identification.<br><br><b>Key features:</b><br>• Regressors \(x_i\) do NOT vary across alternatives (individual characteristics only)<br>• Parameters \(\beta_j\) DO vary across alternatives<br>• Assumes errors \(\epsilon_j\) are iid Type I extreme value<br>• Imposes <b>IIA</b> (Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives)<br><br><b>Marginal effects:</b> \(\frac{\partial p_{ij}}{\partial x_i} = p_{ij}(\beta_j - \bar{\beta}_i)\) where \(\bar{\beta}_i = \sum_l p_{il}\beta_l\). Sign not necessarily given by sign of \(\beta_j\)!	DiscreteChoice::Multinomial
What is the Conditional Logit (CL) model and how does it differ from MNL?	\(\Pr(y_i = j|x_i) = \frac{e^{x_{ij}'\beta}}{\sum_{l=1}^m e^{x_{il}'\beta}}\)<br><br><b>Key differences from MNL:</b><br>• Regressors \(x_{ij}\) DO vary across alternatives (attributes of alternatives)<br>• Parameters \(\beta\) do NOT vary across alternatives (common coefficient)<br><br><b>Example:</b> Transportation choice with travel time and cost varying by mode.<br><br><b>Marginal effects:</b> \(\frac{\partial p_{ij}}{\partial x_{ik}} = p_{ij}(\delta_{jk} - p_{ik})\beta\)<br><br>Positive coefficient → increasing attribute for alternative \(k\) increases probability of \(k\) and decreases all others.<br><br>Also imposes IIA. Same functional form as MNL but different interpretation.	DiscreteChoice::Multinomial
What is the IIA assumption and why is it problematic? (Red bus/blue bus)	<b>IIA:</b> The relative probability of choosing any two alternatives is independent of what other alternatives are available:<br>\(\frac{P(y=j)}{P(y=l)} = e^{x_i'(\beta_j - \beta_l)}\) (MNL) or \(e^{(x_{ij}-x_{il})'\beta}\) (CL)<br><br><b>McFadden's example:</b><br>• Initial choices: car (0.5) and red bus (0.5). Ratio = 1.<br>• Add blue bus. IIA → each mode gets probability 1/3.<br>• But realistically: car should stay at 0.5, red and blue bus split 0.25 each.<br>• IIA forces car probability down from 0.5 to 0.33 — unrealistic!<br><br><b>Root cause:</b> iid errors across ALL alternatives. If two alternatives are similar (red/blue bus), their errors should be correlated, but MNL/CL can't capture this.	DiscreteChoice::IIA
How does the Nested Logit model relax IIA?	Groups alternatives into <b>nests</b> (limbs). Within nests, IIA holds. Across nests, it doesn't.<br><br>Utility: \(U_{jk} = V_{jk} + \varepsilon_{jk}\) for branch \(k\) of limb \(j\).<br><br>Joint probability factors: \(p_{jk} = p_j \times p_{k|j}\)<br><br>\(p_{k|j} = \frac{e^{x_{jk}'\beta_j/\rho_j}}{\sum_l e^{x_{jl}'\beta_j/\rho_j}}\), \(p_j = \frac{e^{z_j'\alpha + \rho_j I_j}}{\sum_m e^{z_m'\alpha + \rho_m I_m}}\)<br><br>where \(I_j = \ln\sum_l e^{x_{jl}'\beta_j/\rho_j}\) is the <b>inclusive value</b> and \(\rho_j = \sqrt{1-\text{Corr}(\varepsilon_{jk}, \varepsilon_{jl})}\).<br><br>\(\rho_j = 1\) → independence within nest → collapses to MNL.<br>\(\rho_j < 1\) → correlation within nest → IIA relaxed across nests.<br><br>Nests MNL/CL as special cases → imposes FEWER restrictions.	DiscreteChoice::NestedLogit
What is the Ordered Probit/Logit model?	For ordered dependent variable \(y \in \{1, 2, \ldots, m\}\):<br><br>Latent variable: \(y_i^* = x_i'\beta + u_i\), \(u_i \sim N(0,1)\)<br><br>Observation rule with cutoffs \(\alpha_0 = -\infty < \alpha_1 < \ldots < \alpha_{m-1} < \alpha_m = \infty\):<br>\(y_i = j\) if \(\alpha_{j-1} < y_i^* \leq \alpha_j\)<br><br><b>Response probabilities:</b><br>\(\Pr(y_i = 1|x_i) = \Phi(\alpha_1 - x_i'\beta)\)<br>\(\Pr(y_i = j|x_i) = \Phi(\alpha_j - x_i'\beta) - \Phi(\alpha_{j-1} - x_i'\beta)\)<br>\(\Pr(y_i = m|x_i) = 1 - \Phi(\alpha_{m-1} - x_i'\beta)\)<br><br>Estimates \(K + (m-1)\) parameters vs \((m-1)(K+1)\) in MNL — more parsimonious but assumes ordering.<br><br><b>Marginal effects:</b> \(\frac{\partial \Pr(y=j)}{\partial x} = [F'(\alpha_{j-1} - x'\beta) - F'(\alpha_j - x'\beta)]\beta\). Sign can go either way!	DiscreteChoice::Ordered
What is the difference between censoring and truncation?	<b>Censoring:</b> Information on the dependent variable is incomplete, but data on regressors is kept for ALL observations.<br>Example: earnings top-coded at \$100,000 — you know someone earns ≥\$100K but not how much. All individuals are in the sample.<br><br><b>Truncation:</b> Observations are completely lost — both \(y\) and \(x\) are missing.<br>Example: only low-income individuals sampled. High-income people not observed at all.<br><br>Truncation entails <b>greater information loss</b> than censoring.<br><br><b>Censoring from below:</b> \(y = y^*\) if \(y^* > L\), else \(y = L\)<br><b>Censoring from above:</b> \(y = y^*\) if \(y^* < U\), else \(y = U\)<br><br>OLS on censored/truncated data → inconsistent (conditional means are nonlinear even if population mean is linear).	Tobit::Basics
Derive the Tobit model likelihood function.	Latent variable: \(y^* = x'\beta + \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\). Censored at 0:<br>\(y = y^*\) if \(y^* > 0\), else \(y = 0\).<br><br><b>For \(y > 0\):</b> density = \(f^*(y|x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(y-x'\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\)<br><br><b>For \(y = 0\):</b> probability = \(F^*(0|x) = \Pr(y^* \leq 0) = \Phi(-x'\beta/\sigma) = 1 - \Phi(x'\beta/\sigma)\)<br><br>Let \(d_i = \mathbf{1}[y_i > 0]\). Combined density: \(f(y|x) = f^*(y|x)^d \cdot F^*(0|x)^{1-d}\)<br><br>Log-likelihood:<br>\(\ell = \sum_{i=1}^N \left\{d_i\left[-\frac{1}{2}\ln 2\pi - \frac{1}{2}\ln\sigma^2 - \frac{(y_i-x_i'\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right] + (1-d_i)\ln\left[1-\Phi\left(\frac{x_i'\beta}{\sigma}\right)\right]\right\}\)<br><br>Two parts: normal density for uncensored obs + probability mass for censored obs.	Tobit::MLE
What are the three types of marginal effects in the Tobit model?	Let \(w = x'\beta/\sigma\):<br><br>1. <b>Latent variable:</b> \(\frac{\partial E(y^*|x)}{\partial x} = \beta\)<br>Simple — same as OLS interpretation.<br><br>2. <b>Censored (at 0):</b> \(\frac{\partial E(y|x)}{\partial x} = \Phi(w)\beta\)<br>Tobit coefficients × probability of being uncensored. <b>This is usually the one of interest.</b><br><br>3. <b>Truncated (at 0):</b> \(\frac{\partial E(y|y>0,x)}{\partial x} = [1 - w\lambda(w) - \lambda(w)^2]\beta\)<br>Effect conditional on being observed.<br><br>where \(\lambda(w) = \phi(w)/\Phi(w)\) is the inverse Mills ratio.	Tobit::MarginalEffects
What is the inverse Mills ratio and why does it appear in selection models?	\(\lambda(z) = \frac{\phi(z)}{\Phi(z)}\)<br><br><b>Origin:</b> It's the conditional expectation of a truncated standard normal. If \(\varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\):<br>\(E(\varepsilon | \varepsilon > -c) = \sigma\frac{\phi(c/\sigma)}{\Phi(c/\sigma)} = \sigma\lambda(c/\sigma)\)<br><br><b>Why it appears:</b> When you estimate a model on a selected sample (e.g., only workers), the conditional mean includes \(\lambda\) as an omitted variable. Omitting it biases OLS.<br><br><b>In the Tobit context:</b><br>\(E(y^*|x, y^* > 0) = x'\beta + \sigma\lambda(x'\beta/\sigma)\)<br><br>Running OLS only on uncensored observations omits the \(\lambda\) term, creating correlation between the error and regressors → inconsistency.	Tobit::InverseMillsRatio
Describe the Bivariate Sample Selection Model (Type 2 Tobit / Heckman model).	Two latent equations:<br><b>Participation:</b> \(y_1^* = x_1'\beta_1 + \varepsilon_1\), observe \(y_1 = \mathbf{1}[y_1^* > 0]\)<br><b>Outcome:</b> \(y_2^* = x_2'\beta_2 + \varepsilon_2\), observe \(y_2 = y_2^*\) only if \(y_1^* > 0\)<br><br>Joint normality: \(\binom{\varepsilon_1}{\varepsilon_2} \sim N\left(\binom{0}{0}, \begin{pmatrix}1 & \sigma_{12}\\\sigma_{12} & \sigma_2^2\end{pmatrix}\right)\)<br>(\(\sigma_1^2 = 1\) normalized since only sign of \(y_1^*\) observed.)<br><br><b>Key equation:</b><br>\(E[y_2|x, y_1^* > 0] = x_2'\beta_2 + \sigma_{12}\lambda(x_1'\beta_1)\)<br><br>OLS on observed \(y_2\) is inconsistent unless \(\sigma_{12} = 0\) (no correlation between selection and outcome errors).<br><br>Simple Tobit is the special case where \(y_1^* = y_2^*\).	Tobit::Selection
Describe the Heckman two-step estimator (Heckit).	<b>Step 1:</b> Estimate a probit for participation:<br>\(\Pr(y_1^* > 0|x_1) = \Phi(x_1'\beta_1)\)<br>Get consistent \(\hat{\beta}_1\). Compute the inverse Mills ratio for everyone:<br>\(\hat{\lambda}_i = \frac{\phi(x_{1i}'\hat{\beta}_1)}{\Phi(x_{1i}'\hat{\beta}_1)}\)<br><br><b>Step 2:</b> Run OLS on the selected sample:<br>\(y_{2i} = x_{2i}'\beta_2 + \sigma_{12}\hat{\lambda}(x_{1i}'\hat{\beta}_1) + v_i\)<br><br>Including \(\hat{\lambda}\) corrects for the omitted variable that caused selection bias.<br><br><b>Test:</b> \(\sigma_{12} = 0\)? If not rejected → no selection bias, OLS is fine.<br><br><b>Standard errors</b> must account for heteroskedasticity AND the fact that \(\hat{\beta}_1\) from Step 1 is estimated.<br><br><b>Heckit vs full MLE:</b> Less efficient under joint normality, but simpler, more robust, and relies on weaker assumptions (\(E[\varepsilon_2|\varepsilon_1] = \delta\varepsilon_1\) suffices).	Tobit::Heckman
What is the identification problem in the Heckman model and how is it solved?	Without exclusion restrictions (\(x_1 = x_2\)):<br><br>\(E[y_2|y_1^*>0] \approx x_2'\beta_2 + a + bx_2'\beta_1\)<br><br>because \(\lambda(\cdot)\) is approximately linear over a wide range. This creates severe <b>multicollinearity</b> — hard to distinguish the selection correction from the outcome equation regressors.<br><br><b>Solution:</b> Include at least one variable in the participation equation (\(x_1\)) that is excluded from the outcome equation (\(x_2\)) — an <b>exclusion restriction</b>.<br><br>This variable must:<br>1. Affect participation (\(\beta_1 \neq 0\) for this variable)<br>2. Not directly affect the outcome (\(\beta_2 = 0\) for this variable)<br><br>Example: distance to job training center affects participation but not earnings directly.<br><br>Without exclusion restrictions, identification relies solely on functional form (normality) — fragile.	Tobit::Identification
What model should you use for: (1) censored data, (2) truncated data, (3) selected sample?	1. <b>Censored data</b> (y top/bottom-coded but all obs present): <b>Tobit / Censored regression</b>. Left censoring: \(y = \max(0, y^*)\). MLE with mixed discrete-continuous density.<br><br>2. <b>Truncated data</b> (some obs completely missing): <b>Truncated regression</b>. Density is \(f^*(y|x)/[1-F^*(L|x)]\). MLE on the truncated density. Greater information loss.<br><br>3. <b>Selected sample</b> (outcome observed only for a non-random subset): <b>Heckman selection model</b>. Separate participation and outcome equations. Correct with inverse Mills ratio or full MLE.<br><br><b>Key distinction:</b> Tobit has one latent variable driving both censoring and outcome. Heckman has two (possibly correlated) latent variables — selection and outcome can be driven by different factors.	Tobit::Summary
For a censored model, derive the truncated mean \(E[y_i|x_i, y_i < U]\) when censoring is from above at \(U\).	Latent: \(y^* = x'\beta + \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\).<br><br>\(E[y|x, y < U] = E[y^*|x, y^* < U] = x'\beta + E[\varepsilon|x, \varepsilon < U - x'\beta]\)<br><br>For truncation from above, \(E[\varepsilon|\varepsilon < c] = -\sigma\frac{\phi(c/\sigma)}{\Phi(c/\sigma)}\):<br><br>\(= x'\beta - \sigma\frac{\phi\left(\frac{U-x'\beta}{\sigma}\right)}{\Phi\left(\frac{U-x'\beta}{\sigma}\right)}\)<br><br>Equivalently (by symmetry of the normal):<br>\(= x'\beta - \sigma\frac{\phi\left(\frac{x'\beta-U}{\sigma}\right)}{1-\Phi\left(\frac{x'\beta-U}{\sigma}\right)}\)<br><br><b>Both expressions are equivalent</b> (answer d on the sample exam). Derive \(E(H|x) = \Phi(w)(x'\beta) + \sigma\phi(w)\) for the Tobit censored mean and the marginal effect.	\(E(H|x) = \Pr(H^*>0|x) \times E(H^*|x, H^*>0)\)<br><br>\(= \Phi(w) \times [x'\beta + \sigma\lambda(w)]\) where \(w = x'\beta/\sigma\)<br><br>\(= \Phi(w)(x'\beta) + \sigma\phi(w)\) (using \(\Phi(w)\lambda(w) = \phi(w)\))<br><br>Differentiate w.r.t. \(x\):<br>\(\frac{\partial E(H|x)}{\partial x} = \beta\Phi(w) + \frac{\beta}{\sigma}\phi(w)(x'\beta) + \sigma\frac{\beta}{\sigma}\phi'(w)\)<br><br>Using \(\phi'(z) = -z\phi(z)\):<br>\(= \beta\Phi(w) + \frac{\beta}{\sigma}\phi(w)(x'\beta) - \frac{\beta}{\sigma}w\sigma\phi(w)\)<br><br>Last two terms cancel (\(w = x'\beta/\sigma\)):<br><br>\(\boxed{\frac{\partial E(H|x)}{\partial x} = \beta\Phi(w)}\)<br><br>Marginal effect = coefficient × probability of working.
