You have survey data on individuals' attitudes toward immigration, recorded on a scale from 1 (very negative) to 4 (very positive). You also have income, education, gender, and age. Which model should you use?	<b>Ordered Probit (or Ordered Logit).</b><br><br>The dependent variable has a natural ordering (1 < 2 < 3 < 4) but the differences between categories are not meaningful in a cardinal sense (the gap between 1 and 2 isn't necessarily the same as between 3 and 4).<br><br><b>Why not MNL?</b> MNL treats categories as unordered — it would ignore the ordering information, wasting efficiency and estimating more parameters than necessary.<br><br><b>Why not OLS?</b> Treats the ordinal scale as cardinal. Marginal effects would be constant, which is unrealistic for bounded ordered outcomes.<br><br><b>Why not Logit/Probit?</b> Those are for binary outcomes. You have 4 categories. You survey people who recently visited a public event (exhibition, theatre, concert, football match). You have individual characteristics (income, education, gender, age). Which model do you use to analyze event choice?	<b>Multinomial Logit (MNL).</b><br><br>The dependent variable is unordered categorical with multiple alternatives. Individual characteristics (not alternative attributes) are the regressors.<br><br><b>Why not Conditional Logit?</b> CL requires alternative-varying regressors (e.g., ticket price, travel time for each event). You only have individual characteristics.<br><br><b>Why not Ordered Probit?</b> There's no natural ordering among exhibition, theatre, concert, football.<br><br><b>Main limitation:</b> IIA — adding a new event type would proportionally steal share from ALL existing alternatives, which may be unrealistic if some events are closer substitutes. Same event-choice setup, but now you also have data on each event's characteristics (ticket price, distance, duration). Which model?	<b>Mixed Logit</b> (combining MNL and CL features).<br><br>\(p_{ij} = \frac{e^{x_{ij}'\beta + w_i'\gamma_j}}{\sum_l e^{x_{il}'\beta + w_i'\gamma_l}}\)<br><br>\(x_{ij}\) = alternative-varying attributes (price, distance) with common \(\beta\)<br>\(w_i\) = individual characteristics (age, income) with alternative-specific \(\gamma_j\)<br><br>If you only had event characteristics → Conditional Logit.<br>If you only had individual characteristics → MNL.<br>Both → Mixed Logit. You estimate a Probit model for employment and get \(\hat{\beta}_{education} = 0.35\). Can you say "one more year of education increases the probability of employment by 35 percentage points"?	<b>Absolutely not.</b><br><br>In Probit (and Logit), the coefficients are NOT marginal effects. The marginal effect is:<br>\(\frac{\partial \Pr(y=1|x)}{\partial x_j} = \phi(x'\beta)\beta_j\)<br><br>The \(\phi(x'\beta)\) scaling factor depends on the evaluation point — it varies across individuals. So 0.35 is the effect on the latent index \(x'\beta\), not on the probability.<br><br><b>What to report instead:</b><br>1. <b>Average Marginal Effect (AME):</b> \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \phi(x_i'\hat{\beta})\hat{\beta}_j\) — average the ME across all observations<br>2. <b>Marginal Effect at the Mean (MEM):</b> \(\phi(\bar{x}'\hat{\beta})\hat{\beta}_j\) — evaluate at sample means<br><br>AME is generally preferred as it doesn't assume a "representative" individual.	Scenarios::Interpretation
You estimate an MNL for occupational choice (3 occupations) and get \(\hat{\beta}_{2,age} = 0.08\) (coefficient on age for occupation 2 vs. base). Is the marginal effect of age on \(\Pr(y=2)\) necessarily positive?	<b>Not necessarily.</b><br><br>In the MNL, the marginal effect is:<br>\(\frac{\partial p_{ij}}{\partial x_i} = p_{ij}(\beta_j - \bar{\beta}_i)\)<br><br>where \(\bar{\beta}_i = \sum_l p_{il}\beta_l\) is the probability-weighted average of ALL coefficients across alternatives.<br><br>Even though \(\beta_{2,age} = 0.08 > 0\), if the weighted average \(\bar{\beta}_{i,age}\) exceeds 0.08, the marginal effect on occupation 2 is <b>negative</b>.<br><br><b>Key lesson:</b> In multinomial models, you cannot determine the sign of the marginal effect from the sign of the coefficient alone. Always compute and report marginal effects explicitly.	Scenarios::Interpretation
In a Conditional Logit for transport mode, you get \(\hat{\beta}_{cost} = -0.05\). If bus cost increases by \$1, what happens to probabilities?	The marginal effect of changing cost for alternative \(k\) on the probability of alternative \(j\):<br><br>\(\frac{\partial p_{ij}}{\partial x_{ik}} = p_{ij}(\delta_{jk} - p_{ik})\beta\)<br><br><b>If \(j = k\) (bus):</b> \(\frac{\partial p_{bus}}{\partial cost_{bus}} = p_{bus}(1 - p_{bus})(-0.05) < 0\)<br>Bus probability decreases. ✓<br><br><b>If \(j \neq k\) (car):</b> \(\frac{\partial p_{car}}{\partial cost_{bus}} = p_{car}(0 - p_{bus})(-0.05) = 0.05 \cdot p_{car} \cdot p_{bus} > 0\)<br>Car probability increases. ✓<br><br><b>IIA problem:</b> The increase in car probability is proportional to \(p_{car} \cdot p_{bus}\). ALL other modes gain proportionally to their current share — IIA doesn't allow some modes to be closer substitutes than others.	Scenarios::Interpretation
You run a Hausman test comparing FE and RE estimators and get a p-value of 0.401. What do you conclude?	<b>Fail to reject \(H_0: E(f_i|X_i) = 0\).</b><br><br>At standard significance levels (5% or 10%), 0.401 is far from rejection. The difference between FE and RE estimates is not statistically significant.<br><br><b>Implication:</b> The RE assumptions appear valid. Use the <b>RE estimator</b> because:<br>1. It's more efficient than FE<br>2. It identifies effects of time-invariant variables (which FE cannot)<br><br><b>Caveats:</b><br>• Failure to reject ≠ proof that RE is correct. Could be low power.<br>• The test only compares coefficients on time-varying regressors.<br>• If you have strong theoretical reasons to believe \(f_i\) is correlated with \(x_{it}\), you might still prefer FE despite the test result.	Scenarios::Testing
You run a Hausman test and get a p-value of 0.003. What do you conclude?	<b>Reject \(H_0: E(f_i|X_i) = 0\).</b><br><br>Strong evidence that the unobserved effect \(f_i\) is correlated with the regressors. The RE estimator is <b>inconsistent</b>.<br><br><b>Action:</b> Use the <b>FE estimator</b>. It remains consistent whether or not \(f_i\) is correlated with \(x_{it}\).<br><br><b>Trade-off:</b> You lose the ability to estimate coefficients on time-invariant variables (gender, ethnicity, etc.) and you lose efficiency. But consistency trumps efficiency — a biased estimator that's precise is worse than an unbiased one that's noisier.	Scenarios::Testing
You estimate a dynamic panel model \(y_{it} = \alpha y_{i,t-1} + x_{it}'\beta + f_i + u_{it}\) using FE and find \(\hat{\alpha} = 0.15\). Your colleague says this is likely biased. Is she right? In which direction?	<b>She's right — the FE estimate is downward biased</b> (Nickell bias).<br><br>The within transformation creates: \((y_{it} - \bar{y}_i) = \alpha(y_{i,t-1} - \bar{y}_{i,-}) + (u_{it} - \bar{u}_i)\)<br><br>\(\bar{y}_{i,-}\) is positively affected by \(u_{it}\) (through the AR structure), making the regressor \(y_{i,t-1} - \bar{y}_{i,-}\) negatively correlated with \(u_{it} - \bar{u}_i\).<br><br>So the true \(\alpha\) is likely <b>higher</b> than 0.15.<br><br><b>What to do instead:</b> Use Anderson-Hsiao (IV with \(y_{i,t-2}\)) or Arellano-Bond (GMM with all valid lags). These are consistent for fixed \(T\), large \(N\).<br><br>The bias vanishes as \(T \to \infty\) but NOT as \(N \to \infty\) — useless in typical micro panels.	Scenarios::DynamicPanel
You estimate an Arellano-Bond model and the AR(2) test on the differenced residuals gives a p-value of 0.02. What does this mean?	<b>Bad news.</b> Reject the null of no second-order serial correlation in the differenced residuals.<br><br>AR(1) in \(\Delta \hat{u}\) is expected mechanically (differencing iid errors creates MA(1) structure). But <b>AR(2) in \(\Delta \hat{u}\) signals AR(1) in the level errors \(u_{it}\)</b>.<br><br><b>Implication:</b> \(u_{it}\) and \(u_{i,t-1}\) are correlated, which means \(y_{i,t-2}\) is correlated with \(\Delta u_{it} = u_{it} - u_{i,t-1}\) through \(u_{i,t-1}\). The \(t-2\) instruments are <b>invalid</b>.<br><br><b>Solutions:</b><br>1. Use deeper lags (\(y_{i,t-3}\) and beyond) as instruments<br>2. Reparameterize the model to eliminate serial correlation, then apply AB to the transformed equation<br>3. Use two-step GMM with the deeper instrument set	Scenarios::DynamicPanel
You have data on household expenditure on leisure activities from a population survey. Some households report zero expenditure. Which model?	<b>Censored regression (Tobit) with left censoring at zero.</b><br><br>The latent variable \(y^*\) = desired/potential leisure spending. Some households would like to spend but face constraints, or simply choose zero. We observe:<br>\(y = y^*\) if \(y^* > 0\), else \(y = 0\).<br><br><b>Why not truncated model?</b> All households are in the sample — even those spending zero. With truncation, zero-spenders would be missing entirely.<br><br><b>Why not OLS?</b> The pile-up at zero makes the conditional mean nonlinear. OLS underestimates the slope because it's pulled toward the mass at zero.<br><br><b>Why not Heckman?</b> Could be used if there's reason to believe the decision to spend anything and the amount spent are driven by different latent variables. Tobit assumes they're the same process.	Scenarios::ModelSelection
You want to estimate the returns to education on wages, but wages are only observed for people who work. Non-workers have no wage data. Which model?	<b>Heckman selection model (Heckit).</b><br><br>This is a classic sample selection problem. You need two equations:<br>1. <b>Participation:</b> \(y_1^* = x_1'\beta_1 + \varepsilon_1\) (work vs. not work)<br>2. <b>Outcome:</b> \(y_2^* = x_2'\beta_2 + \varepsilon_2\) (log wages, observed only if working)<br><br>If \(\text{Corr}(\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2) \neq 0\), OLS on workers only is inconsistent — workers are a selected sample.<br><br><b>You need an exclusion restriction:</b> a variable in \(x_1\) but not \(x_2\). E.g., number of young children (affects participation but arguably not wage offers directly).<br><br>Without an exclusion restriction, identification relies purely on the nonlinearity of the inverse Mills ratio — fragile.	Scenarios::ModelSelection
You estimate a Heckman model and the coefficient on the inverse Mills ratio (\(\hat{\sigma}_{12}\)) is statistically insignificant (p = 0.45). What do you conclude?	<b>No evidence of sample selection bias.</b><br><br>The null \(H_0: \sigma_{12} = 0\) cannot be rejected. This means the errors in the participation equation (\(\varepsilon_1\)) and the outcome equation (\(\varepsilon_2\)) are uncorrelated.<br><br><b>Implication:</b> OLS on the selected sample (e.g., workers only) is consistent. The selection correction is unnecessary.<br><br><b>Practical advice:</b> Report both the OLS and Heckman results. If they're similar and \(\hat{\sigma}_{12}\) is insignificant, OLS is preferred because it's more efficient and doesn't require the exclusion restriction or normality assumptions.<br><br><b>Caveat:</b> Insignificance could reflect weak identification (especially if \(x_1 = x_2\) with no exclusion restriction) rather than genuine absence of selection. You have panel data on firms. A colleague runs Pooled OLS and reports standard errors without clustering. What's wrong?	<b>Standard errors are almost certainly too small (and t-stats too large).</b><br><br>Even if RE1 holds (\(f_i\) uncorrelated with \(x\)), the composite errors \(v_{it} = f_i + u_{it}\) are serially correlated within firms:<br>\(\text{Cov}(v_{it}, v_{is}) = \sigma_f^2 \neq 0\) for \(t \neq s\)<br><br>POLS is still <b>consistent</b> under RE assumptions, but the standard errors ignore this within-firm correlation. They treat each firm-year as an independent observation, overstating the effective sample size.<br><br><b>Fix:</b> Either:<br>1. Use the RE estimator (accounts for the error structure via GLS)<br>2. Use POLS with <b>cluster-robust standard errors</b> at the firm level<br>3. At minimum, report cluster-robust SEs with POLS	Scenarios::Interpretation
You have data on students' test scores and class size. You observe that schools with exactly 41 students have much smaller classes than schools with 40 students due to Maimonides' rule. You want to estimate the effect of class size on test scores. Which design?	<b>Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD).</b><br><br>Running variable: enrollment count (\(X_i\)). Cutoff: 40 students. Treatment: smaller class size.<br><br><b>Sharp or Fuzzy?</b> Likely <b>fuzzy</b> — not all schools above 40 actually split, and some below 40 might. There's imperfect compliance with the rule.<br><br>Estimate the fuzzy RDD with IV: instrument \(D_i\) (actual class size) with \(Z_i = \mathbf{1}[X_i \geq 41]\).<br><br><b>Key concern:</b> Can schools manipulate enrollment to land on a specific side? If parents game the system → density bunching at 41 → continuity violated. Run a McCrary/CJM density test.<br><br>Under monotonicity, identifies LATE for compliers at the cutoff. A training program is randomly offered to unemployed workers. 70% of those offered take it, 10% of those not offered find it elsewhere. You want the effect of actually attending. What's your estimator and what does it identify?	<b>IV/Wald estimator.</b> Instrument actual attendance (\(D_i\)) with random offer (\(Z_i\)).<br><br>\(\hat{\beta}^{IV} = \frac{E[Y|Z=1] - E[Y|Z=0]}{E[D|Z=1] - E[D|Z=0]} = \frac{\text{ITT}}{0.70 - 0.10} = \frac{\text{ITT}}{0.60}\)<br><br><b>What it identifies depends on assumptions:</b><br>• Constant effects: \(\beta_D\) (the common treatment effect)<br>• Heterogeneous + monotonicity: <b>LATE</b> — effect for the 60% who are compliers<br>• One-sided non-compliance (no cross-overs, i.e., 0.10 = 0): <b>ATT</b><br><br>Here there ARE cross-overs (10% take-up without offer), so with heterogeneous effects + monotonicity → <b>LATE</b>.<br><br>The 60% first stage = proportion of compliers. The LATE tells you about people the offer actually moved — not always-takers (take it regardless) or never-takers.	Scenarios::CausalInference
A state raises its minimum wage. You have data on employment in border counties of the treated state and the neighboring untreated state, before and after. Which design?	<b>Difference-in-Differences.</b><br><br>Treatment group: border counties in the treated state.<br>Control group: border counties in the neighboring state.<br>Pre-period: before the minimum wage increase.<br>Post-period: after.<br><br>\(\text{DiD} = (E[Y_{post}|treated] - E[Y_{pre}|treated]) - (E[Y_{post}|control] - E[Y_{pre}|control])\)<br><br><b>Key assumption:</b> Common trends — employment in both sets of counties would have evolved similarly absent the policy.<br><br><b>Using border counties is clever</b> because they're geographically similar, making parallel trends more plausible than comparing the entire state to a random other state.<br><br><b>Check:</b> Plot pre-treatment trends. You want to evaluate a job training program but have no randomization, no cutoff, and no clear policy change over time. You do have very detailed pre-treatment data. You estimate a Tobit model for hours worked and get \(\hat{\beta}_{wage} = 12.5\). Your colleague says "a \$1 increase in hourly wage increases hours worked by 12.5." Is this correct?	<b>No — this is the effect on the latent variable \(y^*\), not on observed hours.</b><br><br>There are three different marginal effects in the Tobit:<br><br>1. <b>Latent:</b> \(\partial E(y^*|x)/\partial x = \beta = 12.5\) — effect on desired hours (including negative)<br><br>2. <b>Censored:</b> \(\partial E(y|x)/\partial x = \Phi(x'\beta/\sigma) \times 12.5\) — effect on actual hours including zeros. <b>This is usually what you want.</b><br><br>3. <b>Truncated:</b> \(\partial E(y|y>0,x)/\partial x\) — effect conditional on working<br><br>If \(\Phi(x'\hat{\beta}/\hat{\sigma}) = 0.65\) at the mean, the censored marginal effect is \(0.65 \times 12.5 = 8.125\). Report this, not the raw coefficient.<br><br>The raw coefficient overstates the actual effect because some of the latent increase falls in the censored region (people who still don't work). You're studying firm investment decisions using panel data. The model includes lagged investment and you suspect firm-level unobservables (management quality) are correlated with the regressors. No serial correlation in \(u_{it}\). Which estimator?	<b>Arellano-Bond (Difference GMM).</b><br><br><b>Why not OLS?</b> \(f_i\) (management quality) is correlated with \(x_{it}\) → omitted variable bias. Also, lagged dependent variable creates further endogeneity.<br><br><b>Why not FE?</b> The lagged dependent variable makes FE downward biased (Nickell bias). With typical firm panels (\(T\) small), bias is severe.<br><br><b>Why not FD alone?</b> First-differencing eliminates \(f_i\) but \(\Delta y_{i,t-1}\) is correlated with \(\Delta u_{it}\) through \(u_{i,t-1}\).<br><br><b>AB solution:</b> First-difference, then instrument \(\Delta y_{i,t-1}\) with \(y_{i,t-2}\) and deeper lags. No serial correlation ensures these are valid instruments.<br><br>Use two-step GMM for efficiency. Check the AR(2) test to confirm instrument validity. If \(\alpha\) is close to 1, consider System GMM instead.	Scenarios::ModelSelection
You run an OLS regression of wages on education, experience, and gender. Then you add firm fixed effects and the education coefficient drops from 0.12 to 0.03. What does Oster's framework tell you?	This is a textbook application of Oster (2019).<br><br>\(\hat{\beta}^S = 0.12\) (short regression, no firm FE)<br>\(\hat{\beta}^L = 0.03\) (long regression, with firm FE)<br><br>Coefficient movement: \(\hat{\beta}^S - \hat{\beta}^L = 0.09\) — adding observables cut the estimate by 75%.<br><br>The bias-adjusted estimate: \(\hat{\beta}^L - \delta(\hat{\beta}^S - \hat{\beta}^L)\frac{R_{max} - R^L}{R^L - R^S}\)<br><br>If \(R^S = 0.15\), \(R^L = 0.35\), \(R_{max} = 0.70\), and \(\delta = 1\):<br><br>\(= 0.03 - 1 \times 0.09 \times \frac{0.70 - 0.35}{0.35 - 0.15} = 0.03 - 0.09 \times 1.75 = 0.03 - 0.1575 = -0.128\)<br><br><b>The bias-adjusted estimate is negative!</b> Under equal selection, unobservables would more than explain away the remaining effect. This is very concerning for a causal interpretation of education's effect on wages.	Scenarios::Interpretation
You have a model: \(y_{it} = \alpha y_{i,t-1} + x_{it}'\beta + \gamma z_i + u_{it}\). \(\alpha = 0\), \(z_i\) is observed, \(\text{Var}(u_{it}|X_i, z_i) = \sigma_u^2\), \(\text{Cov}(u_{it}, u_{is}|X_i, z_i) = 0\). Which estimator is most appropriate?	<b>OLS.</b><br><br>Since \(\alpha = 0\): no dynamic issues (no lagged dependent variable).<br>Since \(z_i\) is observed: no unobserved heterogeneity problem — just include \(z_i\) as a regressor.<br>Homoskedasticity + no serial correlation: OLS is BLUE (Gauss-Markov).<br>Strict exogeneity holds: \(E(u_{it}|X_i, z_i) = 0\).<br><br>No need for FE (nothing unobserved to eliminate), RE (stronger than needed), FD (unnecessary differencing costs precision), or IV (no endogeneity).<br><br>OLS is consistent AND efficient here. Simple is best when the assumptions hold.	Scenarios::ModelSelection
Same model but now \(z_i\) is unobserved, \(\alpha = 0\), and \(E(z_i|X_i) = 0\), \(\text{Var}(z_i|X_i) = \sigma_z^2\). Under what additional assumptions is RE the efficient estimator?	<b>RE is efficient when RE1-RE4 all hold.</b><br><br>\(\alpha = 0\): no dynamic issues. ✓<br>\(z_i\) unobserved with \(E(z_i|X_i) = 0\): RE1 holds. ✓<br>\(E(u_{it}|X_i, z_i) = 0\): RE2 (strict exogeneity) holds. ✓<br><br><b>Additional conditions needed for efficiency:</b><br>Both \(E(z_i|X_i) = 0\) AND \(\text{Var}(z_i|X_i) = \sigma_z^2\) (constant conditional variance).<br><br>The first ensures RE1. Same model, \(z_i\) unobserved, \(|\alpha| < 1\), \(E(z_i|X_i) \neq 0\). You use the FD estimator. What can you say about \(\hat{\alpha}\)?	<b>\(\hat{\alpha}^{FD}\) is downward biased.</b><br><br>After first-differencing: \(\Delta y_{it} = \alpha\Delta y_{i,t-1} + \Delta x_{it}'\beta + \Delta u_{it}\)<br><br>\(\Delta y_{i,t-1}\) is correlated with \(\Delta u_{it}\) because \(y_{i,t-1}\) depends on \(u_{i,t-1}\) and \(u_{i,t-1}\) appears in \(\Delta u_{it} = u_{it} - u_{i,t-1}\).<br><br>Specifically: \(E(\Delta u_{it} \cdot \Delta y_{i,t-1})\) includes the term \(-E(u_{i,t-1} \cdot y_{i,t-1}) = -\sigma_u^2 < 0\).<br><br>The negative correlation between regressor and error biases \(\hat{\alpha}\) <b>downward</b>.<br><br><b>But:</b> The bias vanishes as \(T \to \infty\) (if \(|\alpha| < 1\)) because the problematic term becomes a smaller fraction of the total variation.<br><br><b>Solution:</b> Use AH or AB with \(y_{i,t-2}\) as instrument (valid under no serial correlation in \(u\)). You have \(T = 5\) periods and a dynamic model with one regressor \(x_{it}\) that is only weakly exogenous. You want to estimate using Anderson-Hsiao. What are the dimensions of your instrument matrix \(Z_i\)?	First-differencing loses one period: \(T-1 = 4\) periods of differences.<br><br>But with a lagged dependent variable, the first usable difference is at \(t = 2\) (need \(y_{i,t-2} = y_{i0}\)). With weak exogeneity of \(x_{it}\), we can use \(x_{i,t-1}\) as instrument for \(\Delta x_{it}\).<br><br>For AH, we use ONE instrument per endogenous variable per period. Actually with \(T=5\): differences at \(t=2,3,4,5\) → 4 equations, but the lagged DV needs \(t-2\) so first usable is still \(t=2\).<br><br>AH instrument matrix: \(Z_i\) is \(3 \times 2\) — three usable time periods (losing one more for the extra lag), two instruments per period.	Scenarios::DynamicPanel
You estimate a staggered DiD using a standard TWFE regression and find a treatment effect of -2.5. But all individual event studies show positive effects. What's going on?	<b>The Goodman-Bacon (2021) problem.</b><br><br>With staggered treatment timing and heterogeneous effects, TWFE is a weighted average of ALL pairwise 2×2 DiDs — including "forbidden comparisons" where already-treated units serve as controls.<br><br>If treatment effects grow over time, already-treated units' outcomes are trending up. Using them as controls makes newly-treated units look like they're declining relative to controls → <b>negative</b> 2×2 DiD even though the true effect is positive.<br><br>If these forbidden comparisons have large weight (large groups treated near the middle of the panel), they can flip the overall sign.<br><br><b>Fix:</b> Use Callaway-Sant'Anna, Sun-Abraham, or Borusyak-Jaravel-Spiess. These never use already-treated as controls. Estimate group-time ATT(g,t) and aggregate transparently.	Scenarios::CausalInference

You estimate a probit for loan default and want to do a likelihood ratio test of whether income is relevant. How do you set this up?	<b>LR test:</b> \(LR = 2(\ell_U(\hat{\beta}) - \ell_R(\hat{\beta}))\)<br><br><b>Unrestricted model:</b> \(\Pr(\text{default}|x) = \Phi(\beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{income} + \beta_2 \text{age} + \ldots)\)<br><br><b>Restricted model:</b> \(\Pr(\text{default}|x) = \Phi(\beta_0 + \beta_2 \text{age} + \ldots)\) (drop income, i.e., \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\))<br><br>Estimate both models. Compute log-likelihoods. The LR statistic follows \(\chi^2(1)\) under \(H_0\) (one restriction).<br><br><b>Alternative tests:</b><br>• Wald: \(W = \hat{\beta}_1^2 / \widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\beta}_1) \sim \chi^2(1)\) — only needs unrestricted model<br>• LM (Score): Only needs restricted model — test whether the score is zero at restricted estimate<br><br>All three are asymptotically equivalent under \(H_0\). LR is most commonly reported.	Scenarios::Testing
You have data on leisure spending from a survey, but the survey only includes individuals earning below \$50,000. Which model?	<b>Truncated regression model.</b><br><br>This is truncation, not censoring. Individuals earning ≥\$50,000 are completely absent from the sample — you don't observe their spending OR their income.<br><br>The conditional density for the observed sample is:<br>\(f(y|x, \text{income} < 50K) = \frac{f^*(y|x)}{1 - F^*(50K|x)}\)<br><br>OLS on this sample underestimates the slope because you're missing the upper tail of the income distribution and its associated spending patterns.<br><br><b>Why not Tobit?</b> Tobit is for censoring (all obs present, some values capped). Here the observations are missing entirely.<br><br><b>Why not Heckman?</b> Could work if you model the truncation rule as a selection equation. But if the truncation is based on an observed variable (income) and the cutoff is known, the truncated MLE is more appropriate and efficient.	Scenarios::ModelSelection
You run FE and FD on the same panel data with \(T = 8\). The FE estimate of \(\beta\) is 0.45 and the FD estimate is 0.12. What should you worry about?	<b>Worry about the strict exogeneity assumption.</b><br><br>Under strict exogeneity, both FE and FD are consistent and should give similar estimates (differences attributable to sampling variation and efficiency differences).<br><br>A gap this large (0.45 vs 0.12) suggests strict exogeneity may be violated — specifically, \(u_{it}\) may be correlated with \(x_{is}\) for some \(t \neq s\).<br><br><b>If strict exogeneity fails:</b> FE and FD generally have DIFFERENT probability limits, because they weight the time-series violations differently.<br><br><b>Possible causes:</b><br>• Feedback effects: current shocks affect future regressors<br>• Omitted time-varying confounders<br>• Measurement error with autocorrelation<br><br><b>What to do:</b> Consider IV/GMM approaches, test for serial correlation, check whether a dynamic specification is warranted. You estimate a conditional logit for hospital choice with 5 hospitals. You're considering adding a 6th hospital that is very similar to Hospital 3. Under the CL model, what happens to the choice probabilities?	<b>IIA strikes.</b> The CL predicts that ALL existing hospitals lose patients proportionally to their current shares — not just Hospital 3.<br><br>If before:<br>\(p_1 = 0.30, p_2 = 0.15, p_3 = 0.25, p_4 = 0.20, p_5 = 0.10\)<br><br>CL would predict something like each losing the same proportion. But realistically, Hospital 6 (similar to Hospital 3) should mainly steal from Hospital 3, with minimal impact on dissimilar hospitals.<br><br><b>The CL can't capture this</b> because it assumes iid errors — no correlation in unobserved utility across alternatives.<br><br><b>Solution:</b> Nested Logit. Nest Hospitals 3 and 6 together (similar type). Within-nest IIA is fine (they ARE similar). Cross-nest substitution is more flexible.<br><br>Or multinomial probit (allows arbitrary error correlations) but computationally expensive with 6 alternatives.	Scenarios::IIA
You estimate a probit and get \(\hat{\beta}_{age} = 0.02\) and \(\hat{\beta}_{income} = 0.0001\). Can you compare these to say age matters more than income?	<b>No — raw Probit coefficients are not comparable across variables.</b><br><br>The coefficients are effects on the latent index \(x'\beta\), not on probabilities. They're in the units of the original variables, which differ (years vs dollars).<br><br><b>To compare:</b><br>1. Compute <b>marginal effects</b> (AME) for both: \(\phi(x'\hat{\beta})\hat{\beta}_j\). These are in probability units and comparable.<br><br>2. Or compute <b>standardized coefficients</b>: multiply each \(\hat{\beta}_j\) by the standard deviation of \(x_j\). This gives the effect of a one-SD change in each variable on the latent index.<br><br>3. For the logit specifically, you can interpret via odds ratios: \(e^{\hat{\beta}_j}\) gives the multiplicative effect on the odds per unit change. But comparing across variables still requires standardizing.	Scenarios::Interpretation
Your pseudo R-squared in a probit model is 0.08. Is this bad?	<b>Not necessarily.</b> Goodness-of-fit measures in binary choice models are fundamentally different from linear regression.<br><br>McFadden's pseudo \(R^2 = 1 - \ell_U/\ell_R\) (unrestricted vs intercept-only). Values of 0.2-0.4 are considered very good fits. 0.08 is modest but not unusual, especially with cross-sectional data on individual behavior.<br><br><b>More informative measures:</b><br>1. <b>Percent correctly predicted:</b> If \(\hat{p}_i > 0.5\) predict 1, else 0. Compare to actual. But this can be misleading if classes are very imbalanced.<br><br>2. <b>Statistical significance of individual coefficients</b> matters more than overall fit.<br><br>3. <b>Economic significance of marginal effects</b> — are the effects meaningful in magnitude?<br><br>As the notes say: "goodness of fit is not as important as statistical and economic significance." Don't obsess over pseudo \(R^2\).	Scenarios::Interpretation
