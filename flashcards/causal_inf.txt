What is the fundamental problem of causal inference?	You can never observe the same individual both treated and untreated at the same time. For individual \(i\), you observe either \(Y_{1i}\) (treated outcome) or \(Y_{0i}\) (untreated outcome), never both. The individual treatment effect \(\beta_i = Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}\) is therefore never directly observable.	RCT::Fundamentals
What is the observed outcome in the potential outcomes framework?	\(Y_i = Y_{0i} + \beta_i D_i = \beta_0 + \beta_i D_i + U_i\)<br><br>Where \(D_i = 1\) if treated, \(D_i = 0\) if not. You observe \(Y_{1i}\) when \(D_i = 1\) and \(Y_{0i}\) when \(D_i = 0\).	RCT::Fundamentals
Decompose the naive comparison of means \(E[Y_i|D_i=1] - E[Y_i|D_i=0]\) into the ATT and selection bias.	\(E[Y_i|D_i=1] - E[Y_i|D_i=0]\)<br>\(= E[Y_{1i}|D_i=1] - E[Y_{0i}|D_i=0]\)<br><br>Add and subtract \(E[Y_{0i}|D_i=1]\):<br><br>\(= \underbrace{E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}|D_i=1]}_{\text{ATT}} + \underbrace{(E[Y_{0i}|D_i=1] - E[Y_{0i}|D_i=0])}_{\text{Selection Bias}}\)<br><br>Selection bias = difference in baseline outcomes between those who select into treatment and those who don't.	RCT::SelectionBias
What does random assignment of \(D_i\) imply formally?	\((Y_{1i}, Y_{0i}) \perp\!\!\!\perp D_i\)<br><br>This implies:<br>• \(E[Y_{0i}|D_i=1] = E[Y_{0i}|D_i=0] = E[Y_{0i}]\) → selection bias = 0<br>• \(E[Y_{1i}|D_i=1] = E[Y_{1i}]\) → ATT = ATE<br>• The simple difference in means identifies the ATE<br><br>This is NOT an assumption — it follows mechanically from the randomization process.	RCT::Randomization
Prove that randomization implies \(E[W_i|D_i=d] = E[W_i]\) for any pre-treatment variable \(W_i\).	\(E[W_i|D_i=d] = \sum_w P(W_i=w|D_i=d) \cdot w\)<br><br>\(= \sum_w \frac{P(D_i=d|W_i=w)P(W_i=w)}{P(D_i=d)} \cdot w\)<br><br><b>Key step:</b> Randomization means \(P(D_i=d|W_i=w) = P(D_i=d)\) for all \(w\):<br><br>\(= \sum_w \frac{P(D_i=d)P(W_i=w)}{P(D_i=d)} \cdot w = \sum_w P(W_i=w) \cdot w = E[W_i]\)<br><br>Every pre-treatment variable has the same expectation in treatment and control.	RCT::Randomization
Define ATE, ATT, and ATU. When are they equal?	<b>ATE</b> (Average Treatment Effect): \(E[\beta_i] = E[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}]\) — average effect across the entire population.<br><br><b>ATT</b> (Average Treatment Effect on the Treated): \(E[\beta_i|D_i=1]\) — average effect for those who actually received treatment.<br><br><b>ATU</b> (Average Treatment Effect on the Untreated): \(E[\beta_i|D_i=0]\)<br><br>They are related: \(\text{ATE} = P(D_i=1)\cdot\text{ATT} + P(D_i=0)\cdot\text{ATU}\)<br><br>They are equal when treatment effects are homogeneous (\(\beta_i = \beta\)) or under perfect compliance in an RCT (randomization makes the treated group representative of everyone).	RCT::Estimands
Show that OLS recovers the ATE under perfect compliance with heterogeneous effects \(\beta_i\).	Model: \(Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_i D_i + U_i\). The OLS coefficient converges to:<br><br>\(\text{plim } \hat{\beta}_D = \frac{\text{Cov}(D_i, Y_i)}{\text{Var}(D_i)} = \frac{\text{Cov}(D_i, \beta_i D_i)}{\text{Var}(D_i)}\)<br><br>(since \(\text{Cov}(D_i, \beta_0 + U_i) = 0\) under randomization)<br><br>\(\text{Cov}(D_i, \beta_i D_i) = E[D_i^2 \beta_i] - E[D_i]E[\beta_i D_i]\)<br><br>Under randomization \(\beta_i \perp\!\!\!\perp D_i\), so \(E[\beta_i|D_i] = E[\beta_i]\):<br><br>\(= E[D_i^2]E[\beta_i] - E[D_i]^2 E[\beta_i] = \text{Var}(D_i)E[\beta_i]\)<br><br>\(\boxed{\text{plim } \hat{\beta}_D = E[\beta_i] = \text{ATE}}\)	RCT::Estimation
What is the "rewrite trick" for showing OLS = ATE with heterogeneous effects?	Decompose \(\beta_i = E[\beta_i] + (\beta_i - E[\beta_i])\):<br><br>\(Y_i = \beta_0 + E[\beta_i]D_i + \underbrace{(\beta_i - E[\beta_i])D_i + U_i}_{U_i'}\)<br><br>The composite error \(U_i'\) is mean-independent of \(D_i\) because randomization makes \(D_i\) independent of both \(\beta_i\) and \(U_i\).<br><br>So OLS on \(D_i\) recovers the coefficient \(E[\beta_i] = \text{ATE}\).<br><br>This is the quickest exam proof.	RCT::Estimation
Define no-shows, cross-overs, and the notation for imperfect compliance.	\(Z_i\): assignment to treatment group (randomized)<br>\(D_i\): actual treatment received (endogenous)<br><br><b>No-shows:</b> \(Z_i = 1, D_i = 0\) — assigned to treatment but didn't take it<br><b>Cross-overs:</b> \(Z_i = 0, D_i = 1\) — not assigned but got treatment anyway<br><br>Structural model:<br>\(Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_D D_i + \beta_Z Z_i + U_i\)<br>\(D_i = \pi_0 + \pi_Z Z_i + V_i\)	RCT::Compliance
Why is OLS of \(Y_i\) on \(D_i\) biased under imperfect compliance?	\(\text{plim } \hat{\beta}_D^{OLS} = \beta_D + \frac{\text{Cov}(D_i, U_i)}{\text{Var}(D_i)} = \beta_D + \frac{\text{Cov}(V_i, U_i)}{\text{Var}(D_i)}\)<br><br>People who actually take up treatment differ systematically from those who don't. \(V_i\) and \(U_i\) share common unobserved factors (motivation, ability), so \(\text{Cov}(V_i, U_i) \neq 0\).<br><br>Even controlling for observables won't help if \(V_i\) and \(U_i\) remain correlated conditional on \(W_i\).	RCT::Compliance
Derive the ITT from the structural model with imperfect compliance.	Substitute first stage \(D_i = \pi_0 + \pi_Z Z_i + V_i\) into outcome equation:<br><br>\(Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_D(\pi_0 + \pi_Z Z_i + V_i) + \beta_Z Z_i + U_i\)<br><br>\(= (\beta_0 + \beta_D\pi_0) + (\beta_D\pi_Z + \beta_Z)Z_i + (\beta_D V_i + U_i)\)<br><br>\(= \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 Z_i + W_i\)<br><br>Since \(Z_i\) is randomized: \(E[W_i|Z_i=1] = E[W_i|Z_i=0]\)<br><br>\(\boxed{\text{ITT} = E[Y_i|Z_i=1] - E[Y_i|Z_i=0] = \beta_D\pi_Z + \beta_Z}\)<br><br>Under exclusion restriction (\(\beta_Z = 0\)): ITT = \(\beta_D\pi_Z\), which is attenuated since \(\pi_Z < 1\).	RCT::ITT
What is the ITT and why is it always identified?	The <b>Intention-to-Treat</b> effect is the causal effect of <b>assignment</b> (not treatment) on outcomes:<br><br>\(\text{ITT} = E[Y_i|Z_i=1] - E[Y_i|Z_i=0]\)<br><br>Always identified because \(Z_i\) is randomized — you just compare means across assigned groups, ignoring compliance.<br><br>It's "diluted" relative to the treatment effect because it averages over compliers (who respond), always-takers and never-takers (whose behavior doesn't change).	RCT::ITT
Derive the Wald estimand from the IV estimator formula.	\(\hat{\beta}_D^{IV} = (Z'D)^{-1}Z'Y\)<br><br>Multiply and divide by \((Z'Z)^{-1}\):<br><br>\(= \frac{(Z'Z)^{-1}Z'Y}{(Z'Z)^{-1}Z'D}\)<br><br>Numerator = OLS of \(Y\) on \(Z\) (reduced form)<br>Denominator = OLS of \(D\) on \(Z\) (first stage)<br><br>For binary \(Z_i\), OLS of any variable on \(Z\) gives the difference in conditional means (by the Bernoulli Cov/Var result):<br><br>\(\text{plim } \hat{\beta}_D^{IV} = \frac{E[Y_i|Z_i=1] - E[Y_i|Z_i=0]}{E[D_i|Z_i=1] - E[D_i|Z_i=0]} = \frac{\text{ITT}}{\text{First Stage}}\)	RCT::IV_Wald
Show that under constant effects (A1) and assumptions A2-A4, the Wald estimand identifies \(\beta_D\).	<b>A1:</b> \(\beta_i = \beta_D, \pi_i = \pi_Z\)<br><b>A2:</b> \(Z_i \perp\!\!\!\perp (U_i, V_i)\)<br><b>A3:</b> Exclusion: \(\beta_Z = 0\)<br><b>A4:</b> First stage: \(E[D_i|Z_i=1] - E[D_i|Z_i=0] \neq 0\)<br><br><b>Numerator:</b><br>\(E[Y_i|Z_i=1] - E[Y_i|Z_i=0] = \beta_D\{E[D_i|Z_i=1] - E[D_i|Z_i=0]\} + \beta_Z\)<br>\(= \beta_D\pi_Z\) (using A2, A3)<br><br><b>Denominator:</b><br>\(E[D_i|Z_i=1] - E[D_i|Z_i=0] = \pi_Z\) (using A2)<br><br>\(\boxed{\frac{\beta_D\pi_Z}{\pi_Z} = \beta_D}\)	RCT::IV_Wald
State the four IV assumptions (A1-A4) and explain which follow from randomization and which don't.	<b>A1:</b> Constant treatment effect — a simplifying assumption, not guaranteed by design<br><br><b>A2:</b> Independence of \(Z_i\) — \(Z_i \perp\!\!\!\perp (U_i, V_i)\). <b>Follows from randomization.</b><br><br><b>A3:</b> Exclusion restriction: \(\beta_Z = 0\). Being assigned affects outcomes ONLY through treatment, not through other channels. <b>Does NOT follow from randomization</b> — it's a separate substantive assumption.<br><br><b>A4:</b> First stage: \(E[D_i|Z_i=1] - E[D_i|Z_i=0] \neq 0\). Assignment must actually affect treatment take-up. Testable in the data.	RCT::IV_Wald
Define the four latent types under imperfect compliance. What determines type?	Write \(D_i = D_{0i} + (D_{1i} - D_{0i})Z_i\), so \(\pi_i = D_{1i} - D_{0i}\).<br><br><b>Compliers</b> (\(\pi_i = 1\)): \(D_{1i}=1, D_{0i}=0\). Take treatment iff assigned. The instrument moves them.<br><br><b>Always-takers</b> (\(\pi_i = 0\)): \(D_{1i}=1, D_{0i}=1\). Get treatment regardless. Instrument irrelevant.<br><br><b>Never-takers</b> (\(\pi_i = 0\)): \(D_{1i}=0, D_{0i}=0\). Never take treatment. Instrument irrelevant.<br><br><b>Defiers</b> (\(\pi_i = -1\)): \(D_{1i}=0, D_{0i}=1\). Do the opposite of assignment.<br><br>Type is latent — you can never directly observe it for any individual.	RCT::LATE
What is the monotonicity assumption and why is it needed?	<b>A5 (Monotonicity):</b> \(P(\pi_i = -1) = 0\) — no defiers exist.<br><br>The instrument moves everyone's treatment decision weakly in the same direction.<br><br><b>Why needed:</b> Without it, compliers (\(\pi_i = 1\)) and defiers (\(\pi_i = -1\)) cancel each other out in the numerator of the Wald estimand. You can't separate their effects, and the estimand becomes the uninterpretable WATE. Monotonicity eliminates defiers so the numerator cleanly identifies the complier effect.	RCT::LATE
Derive the LATE under assumptions A2-A5.	\(E[Y_i|Z_i=1] - E[Y_i|Z_i=0] = E[\beta_i\pi_i]\) (from the WATE derivation)<br><br>Decompose by type via iterated expectations:<br>\(= E[E(\beta_i\pi_i|\pi_i)] = E[\pi_i E(\beta_i|\pi_i)]\)<br><br>\(= P(\pi_i=1)\cdot 1 \cdot E[\beta_i|\pi_i=1]\)<br>\(+ P(\pi_i=0)\cdot 0 \cdot E[\beta_i|\pi_i=0]\)<br>\(+ P(\pi_i=-1)\cdot(-1)\cdot E[\beta_i|\pi_i=-1]\)<br><br>Apply <b>monotonicity</b> (A5): \(P(\pi_i=-1)=0\), and the zero kills the never/always-taker term:<br><br>\(= P(\pi_i=1)\cdot E[\beta_i|\pi_i=1]\)<br><br><b>Denominator:</b> \(E[D_i|Z_i=1] - E[D_i|Z_i=0] = E[\pi_i] = P(\pi_i=1)\) under A5<br><br>\(\boxed{\text{Wald} = \frac{P(\pi_i=1)\cdot E[\beta_i|\pi_i=1]}{P(\pi_i=1)} = E[\beta_i|\pi_i=1] = \text{LATE}}\)	RCT::LATE
What does the first stage tell you about compliers under monotonicity?	\(E[D_i|Z_i=1] - E[D_i|Z_i=0] = P(\pi_i = 1)\)<br><br>The first stage directly quantifies the proportion of compliers in the population. A first stage of 0.60 means 60% of the population are compliers.<br><br>This is useful for:<br>1. Assessing external validity — more compliers means LATE is closer to ATE<br>2. Checking instrument strength — weak first stage = few compliers = imprecise LATE	RCT::LATE
Derive the relationship between LATE and ATE.	Under monotonicity:<br><br>\(\text{LATE} = E[\beta_i] + [E(\beta_i|\pi_i=1) - E(\beta_i|\pi_i=0)]\cdot[1 - P(\pi_i=1)]\)<br><br>This comes from:<br>\(\frac{E[\beta_i\pi_i]}{E[\pi_i]} = \frac{\text{Cov}(\beta_i,\pi_i)}{E[\pi_i]} + E[\beta_i]\)<br><br><b>LATE = ATE when:</b><br>1. \(P(\pi_i=1) \to 1\) (almost everyone is a complier), OR<br>2. \(E(\beta_i|\pi_i=1) = E(\beta_i|\pi_i=0)\) (compliers have the same average effect as non-compliers)<br><br>Intuitively: the more compliers, the more representative they are of the full population.	RCT::LATE
Show that with one-sided non-compliance (no cross-overs), IV identifies the ATT.	No cross-overs: \(Z_i=0 \Rightarrow D_i=0\), so \(E[\beta_i D_i|Z_i=0] = 0\).<br><br><b>Numerator:</b><br>\(E[Y_i|Z_i=1] - E[Y_i|Z_i=0] = E[\beta_i D_i|Z_i=1] - 0\)<br>\(= E[D_i \cdot E(\beta_i|D_i)|Z_i=1]\)<br>\(= P(D_i=1|Z_i=1)\cdot E[\beta_i|D_i=1, Z_i=1]\)<br>\(= P(D_i=1|Z_i=1)\cdot E[\beta_i|D_i=1]\)<br>(last step: \(D_i=1\) implies \(Z_i=1\) without cross-overs, so conditioning on \(Z_i\) is redundant)<br><br><b>Denominator:</b> \(P(D_i=1|Z_i=1) - 0 = P(D_i=1|Z_i=1)\)<br><br>\(\boxed{\text{Wald} = E[\beta_i|D_i=1] = \text{ATT}}\)<br><br>And: \(\text{ITT} = \text{ATT} \times P(D_i=1|Z_i=1)\)	RCT::LATE
What does the WATE identify and when does it arise?	\(\text{WATE} = \frac{E[\beta_i\pi_i]}{E[\pi_i]} = E\left[\beta_i \cdot \frac{\pi_i}{E[\pi_i]}\right]\)<br><br>It's a weighted average treatment effect where individuals with higher compliance probability \(\pi_i\) get more weight.<br><br><b>When:</b> Two-sided non-compliance with heterogeneous effects, assumptions A2-A4 hold, but NO monotonicity.<br><br>\(\text{WATE} = \text{ATE} + \frac{\text{Cov}(\beta_i, \pi_i)}{E[\pi_i]}\)<br><br>Equals ATE only if \(\pi_i\) is constant or \(\text{Cov}(\beta_i, \pi_i) = 0\).	RCT::WATE
Complete identification summary: what does each estimator recover under each compliance scenario?	<table border="1" cellpadding="4"><tr><th>Scenario</th><th>Estimator</th><th>Identifies</th></tr><tr><td>Perfect compliance, constant \(\beta\)</td><td>OLS</td><td>\(\beta_D\)</td></tr><tr><td>Perfect compliance, heterogeneous \(\beta_i\)</td><td>OLS</td><td>\(E[\beta_i] = \text{ATE}\)</td></tr><tr><td>No-shows only, constant \(\beta\)</td><td>IV</td><td>\(\beta_D\)</td></tr><tr><td>No-shows only, heterogeneous \(\beta_i\)</td><td>IV</td><td>\(E[\beta_i|D_i=1] = \text{ATT}\)</td></tr><tr><td>Two-sided, constant \(\beta\)</td><td>IV</td><td>\(\beta_D\)</td></tr><tr><td>Two-sided, heterogeneous, no monotonicity</td><td>IV</td><td>WATE</td></tr><tr><td>Two-sided, heterogeneous + monotonicity</td><td>IV</td><td>\(E[\beta_i|\pi_i=1] = \text{LATE}\)</td></tr></table>	RCT::Summary
Why should you use robust or clustered standard errors in an RCT?	<b>Robust SEs:</b> Treatment may affect the variance of \(Y_i\), making homoskedasticity implausible (\(\text{Var}(Y_i|D_i=1) \neq \text{Var}(Y_i|D_i=0)\)). OLS remains consistent but standard SEs are wrong.<br><br><b>Clustered SEs:</b> When randomization is at a group level (e.g., schools) but outcomes are individual (students). Independence within clusters is violated — students in the same school share common factors. Need \(G > 30\) clusters.<br><br><b>Randomization inference:</b> Produces exact finite-sample p-values by repeatedly re-randomizing treatment and computing the test statistic. No asymptotic approximation needed.	RCT::Inference
Why include additional regressors \(W_i\) in an RCT regression? Three reasons.	1. <b>Precision:</b> If \(W_i\) predicts \(Y_i\), including it reduces \(\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_D^L)\) relative to \(\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_D^S)\). Strictly lower variance when \(\beta_W \neq 0\) and \(D_i\) is randomized.<br><br>2. <b>Balance check:</b> Compare \(\hat{\beta}_D^S\) and \(\hat{\beta}_D^L\) — they should be similar since \(\text{plim}(\hat{\beta}_D^S - \hat{\beta}_D^L) = \beta_W \frac{\text{Cov}(D_i,W_i)}{\text{Var}(D_i)}\) and randomization implies \(\text{Cov}(D_i,W_i) \approx 0\).<br><br>3. <b>Consistency is unaffected:</b> \(\text{plim } \hat{\beta}_D^L = \beta_D\) regardless, because randomization prevents omitted variable bias.	RCT::Covariates
What is the continuity assumption in a Sharp RDD?	<b>A2 (Continuity):</b> \(E[U_i|X_i = x]\) is continuous in \(x\) at the cutoff \(c\).<br><br><b>Meaning:</b> As you slide along the running variable toward the cutoff, the average unobservable characteristics don't jump. Only treatment status jumps at \(c\).<br><br>Implies: \(E[U_i|X_i=c, D_i=0] = E[U_i|X_i=c, D_i=1]\), so \(U_i\) is mean-independent of \(D_i\) at the cutoff — like local randomization.	RDD::Identification
What is the "imprecise control" assumption (A2') and why is it useful?	<b>A2':</b> \(P(X_i = x | U_i = u)\) is continuous in \(x\) for all \(u\).<br><br><b>Meaning:</b> No agent type can perfectly control which side of the cutoff they land on. Different types can have different levels of control, but none can guarantee \(X_i \geq c\).<br><br><b>Why useful:</b><br>1. A2' implies A2 via Bayes' rule<br>2. Directs attention to institutional details: "Can people manipulate the running variable?"<br>3. More concrete and falsifiable than abstract continuity<br><br><b>Violation example:</b> Parents gaming school enrollment to land at exactly 41 students.	RDD::Identification
Derive the sharp RDD estimand under homogeneous effects.	Model: \(Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_D D_i + \delta X_i + U_i\), with \(D_i = \mathbf{1}[X_i \geq c]\).<br><br>\(\lim_{x\downarrow c} E[Y_i|X_i=x] - \lim_{x\uparrow c} E[Y_i|X_i=x]\)<br><br>\(= [\beta_0 + \beta_D + \delta c + E(U_i|X_i=c)] - [\beta_0 + \delta c + \lim_{x\uparrow c} E(U_i|X_i=x)]\)<br><br>\(= \beta_D + E(U_i|X_i=c) - \lim_{x\uparrow c} E(U_i|X_i=x)\)<br><br>Apply <b>continuity (A2)</b>: last two terms are equal.<br><br>\(\boxed{= \beta_D}\)<br><br>Without A1 (allowing heterogeneous effects): identifies \(E[\beta_i|X_i=c]\).	RDD::Identification
What is identified in a sharp RDD with heterogeneous effects?	\(\lim_{x\downarrow c} E[Y_i|X_i=x] - \lim_{x\uparrow c} E[Y_i|X_i=x] = E[\beta_i|X_i = c]\)<br><br>The average treatment effect <b>at the cutoff</b>. This is inherently local in two senses:<br>1. Local to \(X_i = c\) — tells you nothing about effects at other values of \(X\)<br>2. Since all units at \(c\) are treated (sharp), it's also the ATT at the cutoff<br><br>Cannot extrapolate without additional assumptions.	RDD::Identification
What is a fuzzy RDD and how does it relate to imperfect compliance in RCTs?	In a <b>fuzzy RDD</b>, crossing the cutoff makes treatment more likely but doesn't guarantee it. \(D_i \neq \mathbf{1}[X_i \geq c]\).<br><br>Directly analogous to RCT with imperfect compliance:<br>• \(Z_i = \mathbf{1}[X_i \geq c]\) = the "instrument" (assignment by the cutoff)<br>• \(D_i\) = actual treatment (endogenous)<br>• No-shows: above cutoff but untreated<br>• Cross-overs: below cutoff but treated<br><br>Requires a <b>first stage (A3)</b>: \(\lim_{x\downarrow c} P(D_i=1|X_i=x) - \lim_{x\uparrow c} P(D_i=1|X_i=x) > 0\)	RDD::Fuzzy
Derive the fuzzy RDD estimand under homogeneous effects and A1-A4.	DGP: \(Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_D D_i + \delta X_i + \beta_Z Z_i + U_i\)<br>\(D_i = \pi_0 + \pi_Z Z_i + \pi_X X_i + V_i\)<br><br><b>Numerator:</b><br>\(\lim_{x\downarrow c} E[Y_i|X_i=x] - \lim_{x\uparrow c} E[Y_i|X_i=x]\)<br>\(= \beta_D[E(D_i|X_i=c) - \lim_{x\uparrow c} E(D_i|X_i=x)] + \beta_Z\)<br>(using A1, A2 for continuity of \(E[U_i|X_i=x]\))<br>\(= \beta_D\pi_Z + \beta_Z\)<br>\(= \beta_D\pi_Z\) (using A4: exclusion \(\beta_Z = 0\))<br><br><b>Denominator:</b> \(\lim_{x\downarrow c} E[D_i|X_i=x] - \lim_{x\uparrow c} E[D_i|X_i=x] = \pi_Z\)<br><br>\(\boxed{\text{Ratio} = \frac{\beta_D\pi_Z}{\pi_Z} = \beta_D}\)	RDD::Fuzzy
What does the fuzzy RDD identify under heterogeneous effects + monotonicity?	Under A2-A5 (continuity, first stage, exclusion, monotonicity):<br><br>\(\frac{\lim_{x\downarrow c} E[Y_i|X_i=x] - \lim_{x\uparrow c} E[Y_i|X_i=x]}{\lim_{x\downarrow c} E[D_i|X_i=x] - \lim_{x\uparrow c} E[D_i|X_i=x]} = E[\beta_i|X_i = c, \pi_i = 1]\)<br><br>This is the <b>LATE for compliers at the cutoff</b> — doubly local:<br>1. Local to the cutoff value of \(X\)<br>2. Local to compliers (those whose treatment status is moved by crossing the cutoff)<br><br>In the Maimonides case: compliers are schools that add a class <b>because of</b> the rule.	RDD::Fuzzy
What is the exclusion restriction in an RDD and when does it fail?	<b>A4:</b> \(\beta_Z = 0\). Crossing the cutoff affects outcomes <b>only through</b> the treatment, not through other channels.<br><br><b>Maimonides example of failure:</b> If crossing enrollment of 40 triggers hiring a new teacher, and teacher competition improves teaching quality independently of class size, then \(\beta_Z \neq 0\).<br><br>The <b>reduced form / ITT</b> does NOT depend on the exclusion restriction: \(\lim_{x\downarrow c} E[Y|X=x] - \lim_{x\uparrow c} E[Y|X=x] = \beta_D\pi_Z + \beta_Z\) is identified regardless. Sometimes the ITT itself is the policy-relevant parameter.	RDD::Assumptions
Describe the local polynomial estimation procedure for sharp RDD.	1. Choose polynomial order \(p\) and kernel \(K(\cdot)\)<br>2. Choose bandwidth \(h\)<br>3. For \(X_i \geq c\): fit WLS \(Y_i = \mu_+ + \mu_{+,1}(X_i-c) + \ldots + \mu_{+,p}(X_i-c)^p + \varepsilon_i\) with kernel weights<br>4. Intercept \(\hat{\mu}_+\) estimates \(E[Y_i|X_i=c]\)<br>5. For \(X_i < c\): fit analogous WLS, intercept \(\hat{\mu}_-\) estimates \(\lim_{x\uparrow c} E[Y_i|X_i=x]\)<br>6. RD estimate: \(\hat{\tau}_{SRD} = \hat{\mu}_+ - \hat{\mu}_-\)<br><br>Normalizing \(X_i\) by \(c\) makes the intercept directly equal the prediction at the cutoff.	RDD::Estimation
Explain the bias-variance tradeoff in RDD bandwidth selection.	\(\text{AMSE}_{\hat{\tau}_p}(h) = h^{2(p+1)}B_p^2 + \frac{1}{nh}V_p\)<br><br><b>Bias term</b> \(h^{2(p+1)}B_p^2\): increasing in \(h\). Larger bandwidth → polynomial approximation worse because the true function curves.<br><br><b>Variance term</b> \(\frac{1}{nh}V_p\): decreasing in \(h\). Larger bandwidth → more observations → lower variance.<br><br><b>Optimal:</b> \(h_{opt} = \left(\frac{V_p}{2(p+1)B_p^2}\right)^{1/(2p+3)} n^{-1/(2p+3)}\)<br><br>\(B_p\) depends on the \((p+1)\)th derivative of the true regression function — estimated with a pilot polynomial of order \(q = p+1\).	RDD::Estimation
What kernel is recommended for RDD and why?	<b>Triangular kernel:</b> \(K(u) = (1-|u|)\mathbf{1}(|u| \leq 1)\)<br><br>Gives maximum weight to observations at the cutoff, linearly declining weight as distance increases, zero weight beyond bandwidth.<br><br>Recommended because with optimally chosen bandwidth, it yields point estimators with optimal MSE properties.<br><br>In practice, results are usually not sensitive to kernel choice. Alternatives include uniform (flat weights within bandwidth) and Epanechnikov (quadratic decay).	RDD::Estimation
What is robust bias correction for RDD inference?	The MSE-optimal bandwidth deliberately allows some bias. Standard CIs ignore this bias and have incorrect coverage.<br><br><b>Solution:</b><br>\(\text{CI}_{rbc} = [(\hat{\tau} - \hat{B}) \pm 1.96 \cdot \sqrt{\hat{V}_{bc}}]\)<br><br>1. Subtract estimated bias \(\hat{B}\) from point estimate (already computed during bandwidth selection)<br>2. Use a <b>larger</b> standard error \(\sqrt{\hat{V}_{bc}} > \sqrt{\hat{V}}\) that accounts for the variability of the bias estimate itself<br><br>From Calonico, Cattaneo, and Titiunik (2014).	RDD::Inference
What are three testable implications of an RDD?	1. <b>Covariate balance:</b> Run RDD on pre-treatment \(W_i\) as outcome. Expect no jump at cutoff. Like balance tests in RCTs.<br><br>2. <b>Density test:</b> Check for bunching in \(X_i\) at the cutoff (McCrary test / Cattaneo-Jansson-Ma). Bunching suggests manipulation, violating A2'.<br><br>3. <b>Sensitivity to covariates:</b> Include \(W_i\) in the main RDD regression. Estimates should not change much — analogous to comparing short and long regressions in RCTs.	RDD::Validity
What is the conditional independence assumption (CIA) for selection on observables?	\(E(U_i|D_i, W_{1i}, W_{2i}) = E(U_i|W_{1i}, W_{2i})\)<br><br>In potential outcomes: \((Y_{1i}, Y_{0i}) \perp\!\!\!\perp D_i | X_i\)<br><br><b>Meaning:</b> Conditional on observables, treatment is "as good as" randomized. Knowing treatment status tells you nothing additional about unobservables beyond what the controls already reveal.<br><br><b>Key distinction from RCTs:</b> This is a pure assumption — not guaranteed by design. You're asserting you've measured every relevant confounder.	Matching::CIA
What is a "bad control" and why is it dangerous even in an RCT?	A <b>bad control</b> is a variable that was itself affected by the treatment. Including it in the regression conditions on a post-treatment outcome, which reopens selection bias.<br><br><b>Example:</b> Training program (\(D_i\)) → white-collar job (\(W_i\)) → income (\(Y_i\)). Controlling for \(W_i\):<br>• Among white-collar workers: untreated got the job without help → higher ability<br>• Among blue-collar workers: treated stayed blue-collar despite training → lower ability<br><br>Result: downward bias <b>even though \(D_i\) was randomized</b>.<br><br><b>Rule:</b> Only control for pre-treatment variables.	Matching::BadControls
Describe Oster's (2019) bias adjustment for unobservables.	Under \(\delta = 1\) (equal selection) and equal relative contributions:<br><br>\(\hat{\beta}^L - [\hat{\beta}^S - \hat{\beta}^L]\frac{R_{max} - R^L}{R^L - R^S} \xrightarrow{p} \beta\)<br><br><b>Components:</b><br>• \(\hat{\beta}^S - \hat{\beta}^L\): coefficient movement (bias from observables)<br>• \(\frac{R_{max} - R^L}{R^L - R^S}\): scaling factor (how much room for unobservables vs. what observables explained)<br><br><b>Small adjustment when:</b> \(R^L \approx R_{max}\) — controls explain nearly everything<br><b>Large adjustment when:</b> Big coefficient movement but small \(R^2\) increase — unobservables likely matter a lot<br><br>\(\delta\) is the degree of proportional selection. \(\delta = 1\) means unobservables equally important as observables for selection.	Matching::Oster
Under the CIA, show that the cell-specific CATE equals the difference in conditional means.	\(\beta_x \equiv E[Y_i|D_i=1, X_i=x] - E[Y_i|D_i=0, X_i=x]\)<br><br>\(= E[\beta_i D_i + U_i|D_i=1, X_i=x] - E[\beta_i D_i + U_i|D_i=0, X_i=x]\)<br><br>\(= E[\beta_i|D_i=1, X_i=x] + E[U_i|D_i=1, X_i=x] - E[U_i|D_i=0, X_i=x]\)<br><br>Under CIA: \(E[U_i|D_i, X_i] = E[U_i|X_i]\), so last two terms cancel:<br><br>\(= E[\beta_i|D_i=1, X_i=x]\)<br><br>And since CIA also gives \(E[\beta_i|D_i, X_i] = E[\beta_i|X_i]\):<br><br>\(\boxed{\beta_x = E[\beta_i|X_i=x] = \text{CATE}}\)	Matching::ExactMatching
How do you obtain the ATE and ATT from cell-specific treatment effects?	Using Law of Iterated Expectations:<br><br><b>ATE:</b> \(E[\beta_i] = E[E(\beta_i|X_i)] = \sum_x \beta_x P(X_i = x)\)<br>Weight by unconditional distribution of covariates.<br><br><b>ATT:</b> \(E[\beta_i|D_i=1] = \sum_x \beta_x P(X_i = x|D_i=1)\)<br>Weight by distribution of covariates among the treated.<br><br>The difference between ATE and ATT is purely the weighting — same cell effects, different weights.	Matching::ExactMatching
How does exact matching differ from a fully saturated regression?	<b>Only difference: weighting scheme.</b><br><br><b>Matching (ATT):</b> \(\sum_x \beta_x \frac{P(D_i=1|X_i=x)P(X_i=x)}{\sum_x P(D_i=1|X_i=x)P(X_i=x)}\)<br>Cells with more treated individuals get more weight.<br><br><b>Saturated OLS:</b> \(\frac{\sum_x \beta_x P(D_i=1|X_i=x)(1-P(D_i=1|X_i=x))P(X_i=x)}{\sum_x P(D_i=1|X_i=x)(1-P(D_i=1|X_i=x))P(X_i=x)}\)<br>Cells with more balanced treatment-control split (closer to 50-50) get more weight — variance-weighted.<br><br>Identical when \(\beta_x\) is constant or \(P(D=1|X)\) is constant.	Matching::Regression_vs_Matching
What is the propensity score and what key result does it enable?	\(p(W_i) \equiv P(D_i=1|W_i) = E[D_i|W_i]\)<br><br><b>Rosenbaum-Rubin (1983) result:</b> If the CIA holds conditional on \(W_i\), then treatment is also unconfounded given the scalar \(p(W_i)\):<br><br>\(E[U_i|D_i, p(W_i)] = E[U_i|p(W_i)]\)<br><br>This reduces the dimensionality problem from matching on a high-dimensional vector \(W_i\) to matching on a single number \(p(W_i)\).<br><br><b>Cost:</b> Requires functional form assumptions for the propensity score model (typically logit).	Matching::PropensityScore
Prove that the CIA conditional on \(W_i\) implies CIA conditional on \(p(W_i)\).	Goal: show \(P(D_i=1|U_i, p(W_i)) = p(W_i)\).<br><br>\(P(D_i=1|U_i, p(W_i)) = E[D_i|U_i, p(W_i)]\)<br><br>\(= E[E(D_i|U_i, p(W_i), W_i)|U_i, p(W_i)]\) (iterated expectations)<br><br>\(= E[E(D_i|U_i, W_i)|U_i, p(W_i)]\) (\(p(W_i)\) is a function of \(W_i\))<br><br>\(= E[E(D_i|W_i)|U_i, p(W_i)]\) (CIA: \(D_i \perp U_i | W_i\))<br><br>\(= E[p(W_i)|U_i, p(W_i)]\) (definition of propensity score)<br><br>\(= p(W_i)\) \(\square\)	Matching::PropensityScore
What are the three main propensity score matching algorithms?	<b>Nearest neighbor:</b> For each treated unit, find the control with closest \(\hat{p}\). Simple but can produce bad matches if nearest neighbor is far.<br><br><b>Radius:</b> For each treated unit, use ALL controls within tolerance \(r\). Better when multiple good matches exist. Some treated units may have no matches within radius.<br><br><b>Kernel:</b> Use ALL controls, weighted inversely by distance to treated unit's score. Uses all information but distant matches still influence estimates.<br><br><b>Tradeoff:</b> NN = low bias / high variance. Kernel = higher bias / lower variance. Radius = middle ground.	Matching::Algorithms
What are the propensity score weights for estimating the ATE and ATT?	<b>ATE weights:</b><br>Treated: \(w_{1i} = \frac{p}{\hat{p}(W_i)}\)<br>Control: \(w_{0i} = \frac{1-p}{1-\hat{p}(W_i)}\)<br><br><b>ATT weights:</b><br>Treated: \(w_{1i} = 1\) (keep as is)<br>Control: \(w_{0i} = \frac{\hat{p}(W_i)}{1-\hat{p}(W_i)} \cdot \frac{1-p}{p}\)<br><br><b>Intuition:</b> Reweighting creates a pseudo-population where treatment is independent of covariates — undoing the selection that observables created. For ATT: make the control group "look like" the treatment group.	Matching::PSWeighting
What is the common trends assumption in DiD?	\(E[\Delta Y_{0i}|D_{i1}=1] = E[\Delta Y_{0i}|D_{i1}=0]\)<br><br>In the <b>absence of treatment</b>, the average outcomes for treated and untreated groups would have evolved the same way over time.<br><br><b>Key implications:</b><br>• Does NOT require equal levels — groups can be permanently different<br>• Does NOT require similar characteristics — just similar trends<br>• NOT invariant to nonlinear transformations (parallel in levels ≠ parallel in logs)<br>• Fundamentally untestable (it's about counterfactual outcomes)	DiD::Identification
Derive the DiD estimand in the two-period case and show it identifies the ATT.	Model: \(Y_{it} = \beta_i D_{it} + \mu_i + \gamma t + U_{it}\)<br><br>Take changes: \(\Delta Y_i = \gamma + \beta_i D_{i1} + \Delta U_i\)<br><br>\(E[\Delta Y_i|D_{i1}=1] - E[\Delta Y_i|D_{i1}=0]\)<br>\(= \gamma + E[\beta_i|D_{i1}=1] + E[\Delta U_i|D_{i1}=1] - \gamma - E[\Delta U_i|D_{i1}=0]\)<br>\(= E[\beta_i|D_{i1}=1] + E[\Delta U_i|D_{i1}=1] - E[\Delta U_i|D_{i1}=0]\)<br><br>Under common trends: \(E[\Delta U_i|D_{i1}=1] = E[\Delta U_i|D_{i1}=0]\)<br><br>\(\boxed{= E[\beta_i|D_{i1}=1] = \text{ATT}}\)<br><br>The \(\gamma\) (common time effect) cancels in the second difference. The \(\mu_i\) (permanent differences) canceled in the first difference.	DiD::Identification
What is Ashenfelter's Dip and why does it threaten DiD?	Trainees' earnings were declining <b>before</b> entering training — a bad year likely caused them to enroll. Their pre-training earnings were unusually low.<br><br><b>Problem:</b> A DiD using the year before training as baseline overestimates the training effect because part of the measured "effect" is just mean reversion — earnings bouncing back to normal regardless of training.<br><br><b>Common trends is violated:</b> The transitory negative shock that caused enrollment means \(E[\Delta U_i|D_{i1}=1] \neq E[\Delta U_i|D_{i1}=0]\) — the treated group would have experienced faster growth even without treatment (recovery from the dip).	DiD::Threats
What is the static TWFE specification and when is it consistent?	\(Y_{it} = \mu_i + \gamma_t + \beta_D D_{it} + U_{it}\)<br><br>Consistent under:<br>1. <b>Homogeneous treatment effects</b> (\(\beta_i = \beta_D\))<br>2. <b>Strict exogeneity:</b> \(E(U_{it}|D_{i1},\ldots,D_{iT}, \mu_i, t) = E(U_{it}|\mu_i, t)\) for all \(t\)<br><br>The proof uses Frisch-Waugh-Lovell: demean within units (removes \(\mu_i\)), demean within time (removes \(\gamma_t\)), then show \(\text{Cov}(D_{it}-\bar{D}_i, U_{it}-\bar{U}_i|\lambda_t) = 0\) under strict exogeneity.	DiD::TWFE
Explain Goodman-Bacon's decomposition and why TWFE fails with heterogeneous effects.	The TWFE estimate \(\hat{\beta}_D\) is a weighted average of ALL pairwise 2×2 DiDs.<br><br>With three groups (never-treated U, early k, late ℓ), there are <b>four</b> 2×2 DiDs:<br>A) Early vs untreated (clean)<br>B) Late vs untreated (clean)<br>C) Early vs late, before ℓ treated (clean — late group is valid control)<br>D) <b>Late vs early, after k treated</b> (PROBLEMATIC — early group is already treated)<br><br><b>The problem with D:</b> If early group's treatment effect is growing, their outcomes trend up faster than counterfactual. Using them as controls makes the late group's effect look smaller or negative.<br><br><b>Weights</b> depend on subsample size and treatment variance. The "forbidden comparison" D can have substantial weight, biasing the overall estimate.	DiD::TWFE_Problems
What is the Callaway-Sant'Anna approach and its key building block?	<b>Building block:</b> Group-time ATT:<br>\(ATT(g,t) = E[Y_{it}(g) - Y_{it}(\infty)|G_i = g]\)<br><br>Average effect at time \(t\) for the cohort first treated at \(g\).<br><br><b>Identified by:</b><br>\(ATT(g,t) = E[Y_{it} - Y_{i,g-1}|G_i=g] - E[Y_{it} - Y_{i,g-1}|G_i=g']\)<br>for any \(g' > t\)<br><br><b>Two assumptions:</b><br>1. Parallel trends: \(E[Y_{it}(\infty) - Y_{it'}(\infty)|G_i=g] = E[Y_{it}(\infty) - Y_{it'}(\infty)|G_i=g']\)<br>2. No anticipation: \(Y_{it}(g) = Y_{it}(\infty)\) for \(t < g\)<br><br><b>Comparison group options:</b> Never-treated (\(G=\infty\)) or not-yet-treated (\(g' > t\))	DiD::CallawayCS
How do you aggregate \(ATT(g,t)\) into event-study parameters?	\(ATT_\ell^w = \sum_g w_g \cdot ATT(g, g+\ell)\)<br><br>Average treatment effect \(\ell\) periods after adoption, across cohorts.<br><br>Weights \(w_g\) can be:<br>• Equal across cohorts<br>• Proportional to cohort size<br><br><b>Advantages over TWFE event study:</b><br>1. Sensible under arbitrary heterogeneity<br>2. Transparent about which units are controls<br>3. Never uses already-treated units as controls<br>4. Pre-treatment coefficients are clean tests of parallel trends	DiD::CallawayCS
Compare the identifying assumptions across all four methods.	<b>RCT:</b> \((Y_{1i},Y_{0i}) \perp\!\!\!\perp D_i\) — guaranteed by design. Strongest.<br><br><b>RDD:</b> \(E[U_i|X_i=x]\) continuous at \(c\) — grounded in institutional details, partially testable. Local.<br><br><b>Selection on observables:</b> \((Y_{1i},Y_{0i}) \perp\!\!\!\perp D_i | W_i\) — pure assumption, untestable. Requires measuring ALL confounders.<br><br><b>DiD:</b> \(E[\Delta U_i|D=1] = E[\Delta U_i|D=0]\) — common trends. Allows permanent unobserved differences (\(\mu_i\)). Only restricts the TIME-VARYING unobservables.<br><br><b>Hierarchy of credibility:</b> RCT > RDD > DiD > Selection on observables (rough guide, context-dependent).	Comparison::All_Methods
When does DiD require a weaker assumption than selection on observables, and vice versa?	<b>DiD weaker:</b> DiD allows \(\mu_i\) to be correlated with \(D_i\) — permanent unobserved differences are fine. CIA requires conditional independence from ALL unobservables.<br><br>Example: motivated people self-select into training (permanent trait) — CIA fails but DiD works if motivation's effect on earnings is time-invariant.<br><br><b>CIA weaker:</b> DiD fails when there are transitory shocks correlated with treatment timing (Ashenfelter's dip). CIA works if you observe all relevant confounders, even time-varying ones.<br><br>Example: people enroll in training after a bad year — common trends fails, but if you observe the bad year's data and control for it, CIA could hold.	Comparison::DiD_vs_Matching
What does each method identify about the treatment effect? (ATE vs ATT vs LATE)	<b>RCT (perfect compliance):</b> ATE (= ATT = LATE since all are randomized)<br><br><b>RCT (imperfect compliance):</b> LATE for compliers (may ≠ ATE or ATT)<br><br><b>Sharp RDD:</b> ATE at the cutoff \(E[\beta_i|X_i=c]\)<br><br><b>Fuzzy RDD + monotonicity:</b> LATE for compliers at the cutoff — doubly local<br><br><b>Selection on observables:</b> ATE or ATT depending on weighting (researcher's choice)<br><br><b>DiD:</b> ATT (effect on the treated group specifically)	Comparison::Estimands
