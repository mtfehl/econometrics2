What is convergence in probability? State the formal definition.	A sequence \(\{S_n\}\) converges in probability to a constant \(c\) if for all \(\epsilon > 0\):<br><br>\(\lim_{n \to \infty} \Pr[||S_n - c|| > \epsilon] = 0\)<br><br>More formally: for every \(\epsilon, \delta > 0\), there exists \(N\) (depending on \(\epsilon, \delta\)) such that for all \(n > N\):<br><br>\(\Pr[||S_n - c|| > \epsilon] < \delta\)<br><br>We write \(\text{plim}_{n\to\infty} S_n = c\) or \(S_n \xrightarrow{p} c\).<br><br><b>Intuition:</b> As the sample grows, the probability that \(S_n\) is far from \(c\) shrinks to zero. The estimator "collapses" onto the constant.	KeyConcepts::Convergence
What does it mean for an estimator to be consistent?	When \(S_n = \hat{\theta}_n\) is an estimator of a parameter \(\theta\), and \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta\), we say \(\hat{\theta}_n\) is a <b>consistent</b> estimator of \(\theta\).<br><br><b>Intuition:</b> With enough data, the estimator gets arbitrarily close to the true parameter value. Consistency is about what happens as \(n \to \infty\) — it says nothing about finite-sample bias.<br><br>An estimator can be biased in every finite sample but still consistent (e.g., IV estimator). Conversely, an unbiased estimator is not necessarily consistent (though it usually is under standard conditions).	KeyConcepts::Convergence
What is convergence in distribution? State the formal definition.	A sequence \(\{T_n\}\) converges in distribution to a random variable \(T\) if for all \(\epsilon > 0\) there exists \(N\) such that for all \(n > N\):<br><br>\(|F_{T_n}(t) - F_T(t)| < \epsilon\)<br><br>at all points \(t\) where \(F_T(t)\) is continuous. We write \(T_n \xrightarrow{d} T\).<br><br><b>Key difference from convergence in probability:</b> The sequence converges to a <b>random variable</b> (a distribution), not to a constant. The variance doesn't vanish — the shape of the distribution stabilizes.<br><br>When \(T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), we write \(T_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) or say \(T_n\) is asymptotically normal.	KeyConcepts::Convergence
Explain intuitively why we multiply \(S_n\) by \(\sqrt{n}\) to get convergence in distribution rather than convergence in probability.	If \(S_n \xrightarrow{p} c\), its variance is shrinking to zero — it's collapsing to a point. To get a non-degenerate limiting distribution, we need to "blow it back up" by multiplying by a function that grows with \(n\).<br><br>The most common choice is \(T_n = \sqrt{n} \cdot S_n\). This works because typically \(\text{Var}(S_n) \approx \sigma^2/n\), so \(\text{Var}(\sqrt{n} \cdot S_n) \approx \sigma^2\) — a constant.<br><br>The rescaled sequence \(\{T_n\}\) converges in distribution to a random variable \(T\) with a non-degenerate distribution, which we can use for inference.<br><br>This is exactly what the CLT does: \(\sqrt{n}(\bar{Y}_n - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\).	KeyConcepts::Convergence
How do you use convergence in distribution to make approximate probability statements about \(S_n\)?	Since \(S_n = T_n / \sqrt{n}\):<br><br>\(\Pr[S_n \leq s] = \Pr[T_n / \sqrt{n} \leq s]\)<br>\(= \Pr[T_n \leq s \times \sqrt{n}]\)<br>\(\simeq \Pr[T \leq s \times \sqrt{n}]\)<br>\(= F_T(s \times \sqrt{n})\)<br><br><b>Intuition:</b> We don't know the exact distribution of \(S_n\) in finite samples. But we know the limiting distribution of \(T_n = \sqrt{n} \cdot S_n\). We use this limit to approximate probabilities for our actual sample, which is the basis for constructing confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.	KeyConcepts::Convergence
What is the relationship between convergence in probability and convergence in distribution?	Convergence in probability implies convergence in distribution, but NOT vice versa.<br><br>If \(S_n \xrightarrow{p} c\) (a constant), then \(S_n \xrightarrow{d} c\) (a degenerate distribution at \(c\)).<br><br>But a sequence can converge in distribution to a non-degenerate random variable without converging in probability to anything — e.g., \(\sqrt{n}\bar{Y}_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\) but \(\sqrt{n}\bar{Y}_n\) does not converge in probability to any constant.	KeyConcepts::Convergence
State Slutsky's Theorem and explain why it's useful.	If \(T_n \xrightarrow{d} T\) and \(S_n \xrightarrow{p} c\), then:<br><br>1. \(S_n + T_n \xrightarrow{d} c + T\)<br>2. \(S_n T_n \xrightarrow{d} c \cdot T\)<br>3. \(T_n / S_n \xrightarrow{d} T / c\) if \(c \neq 0\)<br><br><b>Why useful:</b> You can combine a sequence that converges in distribution with a sequence that converges in probability, and the result inherits the distributional convergence. This is the workhorse for deriving limiting distributions of complex statistics — break them into simple components, find each component's limit, then combine using Slutsky.	KeyConcepts::Slutsky
Give a concrete example of applying Slutsky's Theorem to derive a limiting distribution.	Suppose \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta} - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) and \(\hat{\sigma}^2 \xrightarrow{p} \sigma^2\).<br><br>Then by Slutsky (division rule with \(c = \sigma\)):<br><br>\(\frac{\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta} - \beta)}{\hat{\sigma}} \xrightarrow{d} \frac{\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)}{\sigma} = \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\)<br><br>This is why you can use estimated standard errors in t-tests asymptotically — the consistent estimator \(\hat{\sigma}\) acts like the true \(\sigma\) in the limit.	KeyConcepts::Slutsky
State the Continuous Mapping Theorem (CMT) for convergence in probability.	Let \(g: \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}^j\) be continuous at \(c\). If \(S_n \xrightarrow{p} c\), then:<br><br>\(g(S_n) \xrightarrow{p} g(c)\)<br><br>Equivalently: \(\text{plim } g(S_n) = g(\text{plim } S_n)\)<br><br><b>Intuition:</b> The plim "passes through" continuous functions. If you know the plim of the components, you can apply any continuous transformation and the plim of the result is just the transformation applied to the plim.<br><br>Also holds for convergence in distribution: if \(T_n \xrightarrow{d} T\) and \(g\) is continuous, then \(g(T_n) \xrightarrow{d} g(T)\).	KeyConcepts::CMT
Give an example of the CMT in econometrics.	Suppose \(\hat{\beta} \xrightarrow{p} \beta\) (OLS is consistent).<br><br>By CMT:<br>• \(\hat{\beta}^2 \xrightarrow{p} \beta^2\) (squaring is continuous)<br>• \(1/\hat{\beta} \xrightarrow{p} 1/\beta\) if \(\beta \neq 0\) (reciprocal is continuous away from 0)<br>• \(e^{\hat{\beta}} \xrightarrow{p} e^{\beta}\) (exponential is continuous)<br><br>This is why consistent estimation of \(\beta\) gives you consistent estimation of any continuous function of \(\beta\) — marginal effects, elasticities, etc.	KeyConcepts::CMT
State the Weak Law of Large Numbers (LLN). What conditions are required?	Let \(\{Y_i\}_{i=1}^n\) be iid with \(E(Y_i) = \mu < \infty\) and \(\text{Var}(Y_i) = \sigma^2 < \infty\). Then:<br><br>\(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i \xrightarrow{p} E(Y_i) = \mu\)<br><br>i.e., \(\bar{Y}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu\)<br><br><b>Conditions:</b> iid, finite mean, finite variance.<br><br><b>Intuition:</b> The sample mean converges to the population mean as the sample grows. This is the formal justification for the analogy principle — sample averages consistently estimate population expectations.	KeyConcepts::LLN
Why is the LLN the foundation of consistency proofs in econometrics?	Most estimators can be written as functions of sample averages. For example, the OLS estimator is:<br><br>\(\hat{\beta} = \left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i x_i'\right)^{-1} \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i y_i\)<br><br>By LLN: \(\frac{1}{n}\sum x_i x_i' \xrightarrow{p} E[x_i x_i']\) and \(\frac{1}{n}\sum x_i y_i \xrightarrow{p} E[x_i y_i]\)<br><br>By CMT (matrix inversion is continuous): \(\hat{\beta} \xrightarrow{p} (E[x_i x_i'])^{-1} E[x_i y_i] = \beta\) (under exogeneity)<br><br>This pattern — LLN for components, then CMT to combine — is how virtually every consistency proof works.	KeyConcepts::LLN
State the Central Limit Theorem (Lindeberg-Lévy). What conditions are required?	Let \(\{Y_i\}_{i=1}^n\) be iid with \(E(Y_i) = 0\) and \(E(Y_i Y_i') = \text{Var}(Y_i) = \Sigma_Y < \infty\). Then:<br><br>\(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_Y)\)<br><br>Equivalently: \(\sqrt{n}\bar{Y}_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_Y)\)<br><br>Or approximately: \(\bar{Y}_n \overset{a}{\sim} \mathcal{N}\left(0, \frac{1}{n}\Sigma_Y\right)\)<br><br><b>Conditions:</b> iid, zero mean, finite variance. If \(E(Y_i) = \mu \neq 0\), apply CLT to \(Y_i - \mu\): \(\sqrt{n}(\bar{Y}_n - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_Y)\).	KeyConcepts::CLT
Explain intuitively what the CLT says and why it matters for inference.	<b>What it says:</b> No matter what distribution \(Y_i\) has (could be skewed, bimodal, discrete — anything), the sample mean \(\bar{Y}_n\), when properly standardized, is approximately normally distributed for large \(n\).<br><br><b>Why it matters:</b> It justifies using normal-based inference (t-tests, confidence intervals, Wald tests) even when the underlying data are non-normal. The CLT is why we can construct confidence intervals as \(\hat{\beta} \pm 1.96 \times \text{SE}(\hat{\beta})\) — because \(\hat{\beta}\) is approximately normal by CLT.<br><br><b>The \(\sqrt{n}\) scaling:</b> \(\bar{Y}_n\) converges to \(\mu\) (LLN), so its variance vanishes. Multiplying by \(\sqrt{n}\) stabilizes the variance, giving a non-degenerate normal limit.	KeyConcepts::CLT
How do the LLN and CLT work together in deriving the asymptotic distribution of an estimator?	<b>Step 1 (LLN):</b> Show the estimator is consistent: \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta\). This uses the LLN on sample averages plus CMT.<br><br><b>Step 2 (CLT):</b> Show \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)\). This uses the CLT on the "score" or influence function, plus Slutsky to handle estimated quantities.<br><br><b>Step 3 (Slutsky):</b> Replace unknown quantities in \(\Sigma\) with consistent estimates to get feasible inference.<br><br><b>Result:</b> \(\hat{\theta}_n \overset{a}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\theta, \Sigma/n)\), which gives you standard errors, confidence intervals, and test statistics.	KeyConcepts::CLT_LLN
State the Law of Iterated Expectations (LIE) and explain its intuition.	\(E_Y[Y] = E_X[E_{Y|X}[Y|X]]\)<br><br><b>Intuition:</b> To get the unconditional expectation of \(Y\), you can:<br>1. Compute \(E[Y|X=x]\) for each value of \(x\)<br>2. Average these conditional expectations, weighting by \(P(X=x)\)<br><br>Loosely: "average of averages = overall average" (with proper weighting).<br><br><b>Example:</b> Average income = weighted average of (average income for men) and (average income for women), weighted by the proportion of men and women.	KeyConcepts::LIE
Prove the Law of Iterated Expectations.	\(E_X[E_{Y|X}[Y|X]] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y \, f_{Y|X}(y|x) \, dy\right) f_X(x) \, dx\)<br><br>\(= \int\int y \, f_{Y|X}(y|x) f_X(x) \, dy \, dx\)<br><br>\(= \int\int y \, f_{XY}(x,y) \, dy \, dx\) (since \(f_{Y|X} \cdot f_X = f_{XY}\))<br><br>\(= \int\int y \, f_{X|Y}(x|y) f_Y(y) \, dy \, dx\) (rewrite joint)<br><br>\(= \int y \left(\int f_{X|Y}(x|y) \, dx\right) f_Y(y) \, dy\)<br><br>\(= \int y \cdot 1 \cdot f_Y(y) \, dy\) (conditional density integrates to 1)<br><br>\(= E_Y[Y]\) \(\square\)	KeyConcepts::LIE
Where is the LIE used in the causal inference proofs from this course?	The LIE appears in virtually every derivation:<br><br><b>RCTs:</b> Proving OLS = ATE with heterogeneous effects: \(E[D_i^2 \beta_i] = E[D_i^2 E(\beta_i|D_i)]\)<br><br><b>RCTs:</b> LATE derivation: \(E[\beta_i \pi_i] = E[E(\beta_i \pi_i | \pi_i)] = E[\pi_i E(\beta_i|\pi_i)]\) — decomposing by complier type<br><br><b>Matching:</b> ATE from CATEs: \(E[\beta_i] = E[E(\beta_i|X_i)] = \sum_x \beta_x P(X_i=x)\)<br><br><b>Matching:</b> Propensity score proof: \(E[D_i|U_i, p(W_i)] = E[E(D_i|U_i, W_i)|U_i, p(W_i)]\)<br><br><b>PS weighting:</b> Moving from \(E[Y_i w_i | D_i]\) to conditioning on \(W_i\)	KeyConcepts::LIE
What is the analogy principle?	The analogy principle proposes to estimate population parameters by their <b>sample counterparts</b>.<br><br><b>Examples:</b><br>• \(E(Y_i) \to \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\) (sample mean)<br>• \(E(X_i Y_i) \to \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i Y_i\) (sample moment)<br>• \(\text{Var}(Y_i) \to \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i - \bar{Y})^2\) (sample variance)<br><br>The LLN guarantees these sample analogs are consistent for their population counterparts. The analogy principle + LLN is the fundamental justification for estimation — replace expectations with averages.	KeyConcepts::AnalogyPrinciple
What are the three asymptotic properties of estimators? Define each formally.	<b>1. Consistency:</b> \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta\) for any value of \(\theta\). The estimator gets arbitrarily close to the truth as \(n \to \infty\).<br><br><b>2. Asymptotic normality:</b> \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)\) where \(\Sigma\) is positive semi-definite. In practice we treat \(\hat{\theta}_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \Sigma/n)\) even though this is only an approximation.<br><br><b>3. Asymptotic efficiency:</b> \(\hat{\theta}_n\) is asymptotically more efficient than \(\tilde{\theta}_n\) iff \(\Lambda - \Sigma\) is positive semi-definite, where \(\Sigma, \Lambda\) are their respective asymptotic variance-covariance matrices. The efficient estimator has the smallest asymptotic variance among all consistent, asymptotically normal estimators.	KeyConcepts::AsymptoticProperties
Why do we write \(\hat{\theta}_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \Sigma/n)\) even though this is technically wrong?	\(\hat{\theta}_n\) is generally NOT exactly normally distributed in finite samples, and its variance-covariance matrix is generally NOT exactly \(\Sigma/n\).<br><br>What we actually know: \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)\)<br><br>We <b>treat</b> \(\hat{\theta}_n\) as if \(\hat{\theta}_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \Sigma/n)\) because:<br>1. It's a convenient approximation for large \(n\)<br>2. It lets us construct confidence intervals: \(\hat{\theta} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\hat{\Sigma}/n}\)<br>3. It lets us do hypothesis testing with standard normal / chi-squared critical values<br><br>The approximation improves as \(n\) grows. For small \(n\), exact finite-sample distributions may differ substantially.	KeyConcepts::AsymptoticProperties
What does asymptotic efficiency mean and how is it defined for two estimators?	\(\hat{\theta}_n\) is asymptotically more efficient than \(\tilde{\theta}_n\) if both are consistent and asymptotically normal, and:<br><br>\(\Lambda - \Sigma\) is positive semi-definite<br><br>where \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)\) and \(\sqrt{n}(\tilde{\theta}_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Lambda)\).<br><br><b>Meaning:</b> For any linear combination of parameters, \(\hat{\theta}_n\) has weakly smaller asymptotic variance. It gives you tighter confidence intervals and more powerful tests.<br><br><b>Examples from the course:</b><br>• GLS is more efficient than OLS under heteroskedasticity<br>• Two-step AB-GMM is more efficient than Anderson-Hsiao<br>• Random Effects is more efficient than Fixed Effects (when RE assumptions hold)	KeyConcepts::AsymptoticProperties
Outline the "standard recipe" for deriving the asymptotic distribution of an estimator using the tools from these notes.	<b>Step 1:</b> Write the estimator as a function of sample averages (analogy principle)<br><br><b>Step 2:</b> Apply LLN to each sample average → convergence in probability to population moments → <b>consistency</b> (via CMT for combining)<br><br><b>Step 3:</b> Rearrange into the form \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta} - \theta) = f(\text{sample averages})\)<br><br><b>Step 4:</b> Apply CLT to the key sample average → convergence in distribution to normal<br><br><b>Step 5:</b> Apply Slutsky's Theorem to handle terms that converge in probability (they act like constants in the limit)<br><br><b>Step 6:</b> Apply CMT if nonlinear transformations are involved<br><br><b>Result:</b> \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta} - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)\) → asymptotic normality → feasible inference by replacing \(\Sigma\) with a consistent estimate	KeyConcepts::Recipe
Apply the standard recipe to derive the asymptotic distribution of the OLS estimator.	\(\hat{\beta} = (X'X/n)^{-1}(X'Y/n)\). Substituting \(Y = X\beta + U\):<br><br>\(\hat{\beta} - \beta = (X'X/n)^{-1}(X'U/n)\)<br><br>\(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta} - \beta) = \underbrace{(X'X/n)^{-1}}_{\xrightarrow{p} Q^{-1} \text{ by LLN+CMT}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}X'U}_{\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma) \text{ by CLT}}\)<br><br>By <b>Slutsky</b> (product of plim and distributional convergence):<br><br>\(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta} - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, Q^{-1}\Sigma Q^{-1})\)<br><br>where \(Q = E[x_i x_i']\) and \(\Sigma = E[x_i x_i' u_i^2]\).<br><br>Under homoskedasticity: \(\Sigma = \sigma^2 Q\), so this simplifies to \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 Q^{-1})\).	KeyConcepts::Recipe
