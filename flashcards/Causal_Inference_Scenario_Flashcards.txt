You randomly assign villages to receive a microcredit program. 75% of offered households enroll, 5% of non-offered households find microcredit elsewhere. You observe higher income in the offered group. Can you say the program caused the income increase?	<b>Yes — for the ITT.</b> Random assignment of \(Z_i\) means the difference in means \(E[Y|Z=1] - E[Y|Z=0]\) is a valid causal estimate of the effect of being <b>offered</b> the program.<br><br>But this is NOT the effect of <b>receiving</b> microcredit. That requires IV.<br><br>There are cross-overs (5%), so this is two-sided non-compliance. Under monotonicity:<br>• First stage = 0.75 - 0.05 = 0.70 = share of compliers<br>• Wald = ITT / 0.70 = LATE for compliers<br><br>You CANNOT interpret the simple comparison \(E[Y|D=1] - E[Y|D=0]\) causally — \(D_i\) is endogenous (people self-selected into actual take-up). In an RCT of a drug, everyone assigned to treatment takes the drug, but nobody in the control group obtains it. The estimated treatment effect is 5.2 points. What parameter have you identified?	<b>ATE = ATT = 5.2</b> (under homogeneous effects) or <b>ATE = E[\(\beta_i\)] = 5.2</b> (under heterogeneous effects).<br><br>This is <b>perfect compliance</b>: \(D_i = Z_i\) for all \(i\). No no-shows, no cross-overs.<br><br>Under randomization, \((Y_1, Y_0) \perp D\), so:<br>• Selection bias = 0<br>• ATT = ATE (treated group is representative of everyone)<br>• Simple difference in means = ATE<br><br>No need for IV. No LATE distinction. The cleanest possible case.	CI_Scenarios::RCT
You run an RCT and find that the treatment group has significantly higher baseline education than the control group. What does this suggest and what should you do?	<b>The randomization may have failed.</b><br><br>Under proper randomization, \(E[W_i|D_i=1] = E[W_i|D_i=0]\) for all pre-treatment variables \(W_i\). A significant imbalance in education suggests either:<br>1. Bad luck (possible with small samples — check joint F-test across ALL covariates)<br>2. Randomization wasn't properly implemented<br>3. Differential attrition post-randomization<br><br><b>What to do:</b><br>1. Run a joint test: regress \(D_i\) on all \(W_i\)'s and test joint significance<br>2. Compare short and long regression estimates: \(\text{plim}(\hat{\beta}^S - \hat{\beta}^L) = \beta_W \frac{\text{Cov}(D,W)}{\text{Var}(D)}\). If randomization holds, these should be very close<br>3. Include education as a control — under randomization, this improves precision without affecting consistency<br>4. Report both controlled and uncontrolled estimates for transparency	CI_Scenarios::RCT
An RCT offers free job training. You compute \(E[Y|D=1] - E[Y|D=0] = 8000\) (comparing actual participants vs non-participants). Your colleague claims this is the causal effect. Is she right?	<b>No.</b> This is comparing actual treatment receipt, not random assignment. \(D_i\) is endogenous — people who chose to attend training likely differ from those who didn't (more motivated, different baseline skills).<br><br>\(E[Y|D=1] - E[Y|D=0] = \text{ATT} + \text{Selection Bias}\)<br><br>The selection bias is likely positive (motivated people earn more regardless), so 8000 <b>overestimates</b> the true effect.<br><br><b>The correct approach:</b> Compare by assignment \(Z_i\):<br>• ITT = \(E[Y|Z=1] - E[Y|Z=0]\) — always valid<br>• LATE = ITT / first stage — effect for compliers<br><br>The randomization guarantees \(Z_i \perp (Y_1, Y_0)\), NOT \(D_i \perp (Y_1, Y_0)\). In your RCT, \(\hat{E}[Y|Z=1] = 45000\), \(\hat{E}[Y|Z=0] = 40000\), \(\hat{P}(D=1|Z=1) = 0.80\), \(\hat{P}(D=1|Z=0) = 0\). What does IV identify here?	<b>ATT</b> (not just LATE).<br><br>This is <b>one-sided non-compliance</b>: no cross-overs (\(P(D=1|Z=0) = 0\)). Everyone in the control group stays untreated. But 20% of the treatment group are no-shows.<br><br>ITT = 45000 - 40000 = 5000<br>First stage = 0.80 - 0 = 0.80<br>Wald = 5000/0.80 = 6250<br><br>With no cross-overs and heterogeneous effects, the Wald estimand identifies \(E[\beta_i|D_i=1]\) = ATT, because among the treated there are only compliers (no always-takers possible when \(P(D=1|Z=0) = 0\)).<br><br>Also: ITT = ATT × \(P(D=1|Z=1)\), so ATT = ITT/0.80 = 6250. ✓<br><br>If there WERE cross-overs, you'd need monotonicity and would get LATE instead. Your RCT has both no-shows and cross-overs. Under LATE assumptions, the Wald estimate is 12,000. Can you say the program raises everyone's income by 12,000?	<b>No.</b> The LATE is the average effect for <b>compliers only</b> — people whose treatment status was actually changed by the random assignment.<br><br>It tells you nothing about:<br>• <b>Always-takers:</b> would take treatment regardless — the experiment doesn't vary their treatment, so you can't learn their effect<br>• <b>Never-takers:</b> never take treatment regardless — same logic<br><br>The LATE = ATE only if compliers are representative of the whole population. Specifically:<br>\(\text{LATE} = \text{ATE} + [E(\beta_i|\pi_i=1) - E(\beta_i|\pi_i=0)][1-P(\pi_i=1)]\)<br><br>If the first stage is large (many compliers), LATE is closer to ATE. If it's small (few compliers), LATE could be very different from ATE — and from the effect you'd see if you scaled the program to the full population.	CI_Scenarios::RCT
You randomly assign households to be offered a conditional cash transfer. You want to know: does being offered the program affect children's school attendance, regardless of whether parents actually enroll? Which estimand answers this question?	<b>The ITT.</b><br><br>\(\text{ITT} = E[Y_i|Z_i=1] - E[Y_i|Z_i=0]\)<br><br>This is the effect of the <b>offer</b> (assignment), not of actual enrollment. It captures everything that happens because of the offer: direct enrollment effects, information effects, behavioral responses to knowing the program exists, etc.<br><br>The ITT is:<br>• Always identified under randomization (no additional assumptions needed)<br>• Policy-relevant: a government deciding whether to roll out the program cares about the effect of <b>offering</b> it, since they can't force enrollment<br>• "Diluted" relative to the treatment effect on actual enrollees<br><br>If instead you want the effect of actual enrollment → use IV/LATE. But the ITT is often the more directly policy-relevant parameter.	CI_Scenarios::RCT
You estimate a Wald/IV and find the first stage is only 0.03 (3% compliance). Should you be concerned?	<b>Very concerned — weak instrument problem.</b><br><br>A first stage of 0.03 means only 3% of the population are compliers. Problems:<br><br>1. <b>Statistical:</b> The Wald estimate = ITT/0.03 amplifies any noise in the ITT by ~33x. Estimates will be extremely imprecise with huge standard errors.<br><br>2. <b>Bias:</b> With weak instruments, IV is biased toward OLS in finite samples. Standard asymptotics break down.<br><br>3. <b>External validity:</b> Even if precisely estimated, the LATE applies to only 3% of the population. These compliers may be very unrepresentative.<br><br><b>Diagnostics:</b><br>• Check the first-stage F-statistic (rule of thumb: F > 10, but see Montiel Olea & Pflueger for robust thresholds)<br>• Report the Anderson-Rubin test (robust to weak instruments)<br>• Report the ITT separately — it's always valid regardless of instrument strength	CI_Scenarios::RCT
You claim the exclusion restriction holds in your RCT. A referee argues that being assigned to the treatment group might affect outcomes through channels other than the treatment itself (e.g., Hawthorne effect). How do you respond?	The referee is challenging \(\beta_Z = 0\) — the assumption that assignment affects outcomes ONLY through actual treatment receipt.<br><br><b>The Hawthorne effect:</b> Being told you're in the "treatment group" might change behavior (excitement, extra effort) regardless of whether you actually get treated. If so, \(\beta_Z \neq 0\) and the exclusion restriction fails.<br><br><b>Consequences:</b><br>• ITT = \(\beta_D\pi_Z + \beta_Z\) — still identified, still causal, but now captures both the treatment channel and the assignment channel<br>• Wald = \(\frac{\beta_D\pi_Z + \beta_Z}{\pi_Z} = \beta_D + \frac{\beta_Z}{\pi_Z}\) — biased for \(\beta_D\)<br><br><b>Defenses:</b><br>1. Blinding: if subjects don't know their assignment status, \(\beta_Z = 0\) is more plausible<br>2. Argue the Hawthorne channel is negligible in your context<br>3. Report the ITT and note it's valid regardless of the exclusion restriction — it's the reduced form	CI_Scenarios::RCT
Why can't you use homoskedastic standard errors in an RCT even though treatment is randomly assigned?	Because treatment may affect the <b>variance</b> of the outcome, even though it doesn't affect the <b>mean</b> in a biased way.<br><br>Homoskedasticity assumes: \(\text{Var}(Y_i|D_i=1) = \text{Var}(Y_i|D_i=0)\)<br><br>But a training program might make outcomes more dispersed (some people benefit a lot, others don't) or less dispersed. If so, \(U_i\) is heteroskedastic and conventional SEs are inconsistent.<br><br><b>Solutions (from the notes):</b><br>1. <b>Difference-in-means SE:</b> \(\text{Var}(\bar{Y}_T - \bar{Y}_C) = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_T^2}{N_1} + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_C^2}{N_0}\) — allows different variances in each group<br>2. <b>Robust SEs:</b> Sandwich estimator, always valid<br>3. <b>Clustered SEs:</b> If randomization is at the group level (e.g., schools), cluster at that level<br>4. <b>Randomization inference:</b> Exact finite-sample distribution, no asymptotic approximation needed	CI_Scenarios::RCT
A scholarship is given to students scoring below 50 on a poverty index. You want to estimate the effect on university enrollment. Schools just above and below the cutoff look similar. What design do you use and what must you check?	<b>Regression Discontinuity Design.</b><br><br>Running variable: poverty index \(X_i\). Cutoff: \(c = 50\). Treatment: scholarship.<br><br><b>First determine: Sharp or Fuzzy?</b><br>• If ALL below 50 get scholarships and NONE above do → Sharp RDD<br>• If some below 50 don't take it or some above 50 get it → Fuzzy RDD<br><br><b>Must check:</b><br>1. <b>Covariate balance:</b> Run RDD on pre-treatment variables (parental income, prior GPA) as outcomes. In the scholarship RDD, you notice that the density of the poverty index has a suspicious spike just below 50. What does this mean and what should you do?	<b>Evidence of manipulation.</b> Students (or administrators) may be gaming the poverty score to fall below the cutoff and qualify for the scholarship.<br><br><b>Why this is devastating:</b> If students who manipulate their score to be just below 50 are systematically different from those just above (more motivated, better-connected families), then the composition of who you're comparing JUMPS at the cutoff. You estimate a fuzzy RDD and find the first stage (jump in treatment probability at the cutoff) is 0.35. You're choosing bandwidth for an RDD. With \(h = 5\), you get an estimate of 4.2 (SE = 1.8). With \(h = 20\), you get 2.1 (SE = 0.6). Which do you trust more?	<b>Neither blindly — this illustrates the bias-variance tradeoff.</b><br><br>\(h = 5\): Small bandwidth → low bias (linear approximation is good over narrow range) but high variance (few observations). The wide CI reflects this imprecision.<br><br>\(h = 20\): Large bandwidth → low variance (many observations) but potentially high bias (linear fit may be poor if the true function curves).<br><br>The estimate dropping from 4.2 to 2.1 as bandwidth increases suggests the larger bandwidth is picking up curvature in the regression function — the bias is pulling the estimate down.<br><br><b>Best practice:</b><br>1. Use the MSE-optimal bandwidth from Calonico-Cattaneo-Titiunik (data-driven)<br>2. Report robust bias-corrected CIs: \((\hat{\tau} - \hat{B}) \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat{V}_{bc}}\)<br>3. Show sensitivity to bandwidth choice — if the estimate is stable across a range, that's reassuring<br>4. Consider higher-order polynomial (local quadratic) if curvature is evident	CI_Scenarios::RDD
You run an RDD and include covariates \(W_i\) (parental income, prior GPA). Your estimate barely changes from the specification without covariates. You estimate the ATT of job training using nearest-neighbor propensity score matching. Then you re-estimate using kernel matching and get a very different answer. What's going on?	<b>The two methods weight control observations differently, and with heterogeneous effects this matters.</b><br><br><b>NN matching:</b> Each treated unit matched to its single closest control. Low bias (close matches) but high variance (using few controls per treated). If some matches are poor (sparse regions of common support), individual match quality varies a lot.<br><br><b>Kernel matching:</b> Each treated unit matched to ALL controls, weighted by proximity. Lower variance (uses all information) but potentially higher bias (distant controls get some weight).<br><br><b>If results diverge substantially:</b><br>1. Check <b>common support</b> — are there regions with treated but no controls? NN might match to very distant controls there<br>2. Check <b>balance</b> after matching — which method achieves better covariate balance?<br>3. Try <b>radius matching</b> as a middle ground<br>4. The divergence suggests results are sensitive to methodological choices — <b>be cautious about causal claims</b>	CI_Scenarios::Matching
You estimate a propensity score and find that treated individuals have scores clustered between 0.7-0.95, while controls are clustered between 0.05-0.30. What's the problem?	<b>Poor common support (overlap).</b><br><br>The propensity score distributions barely overlap. For treated individuals with \(p(W) = 0.85\), there are essentially no comparable controls. Any match would be extrapolating far outside the control group's distribution.<br><br><b>Consequences:</b><br>• ATT is only defined in the region of common support<br>• Treated units outside this region must be dropped — but then your ATT isn't representative of all treated<br>• If you force matches anyway, you're comparing apples to oranges<br><br><b>What this really means:</b> Treated and control groups are so different that even conditional on observables, they're not comparable. The CIA might be technically satisfied but practically useless.<br><br><b>Solutions:</b><br>1. Trim to common support region<br>2. Report how many treated units are dropped and their characteristics<br>3. Consider whether a different identification strategy (DiD, IV) might be more appropriate<br>4. This is a sign the CIA may be implausible — if groups are this different on observables, they're likely different on unobservables too	CI_Scenarios::Matching

Covariate balance only confirms that the propensity score model successfully made the groups comparable on measured characteristics.<br><br>There could still be unobserved confounders (motivation, private information, risk preferences) that differ between matched treated and control units.<br><br><b>What balance tells you:</b> The propensity score specification is adequate for the variables you measured. Placebo outcomes (treatment effect on pre-treatment variables should be zero)<br>2. Pseudo-treatments (effect between two control groups should be zero)<br>3. You estimate the effect of training using matching and get ATT = 5,000. Using OLS with the same covariates, you get 4,800. Should these be the same?	<b>Not necessarily — they estimate different weighted averages of cell-specific effects.</b><br><br>Under the CIA with heterogeneous effects:<br><br><b>Matching (ATT):</b> \(\sum_x \beta_x P(X=x|D=1)\)<br>Weights cells by the distribution of treated individuals.<br><br><b>Saturated OLS:</b> \(\frac{\sum_x \beta_x \text{Var}(D|X=x)P(X=x)}{\sum_x \text{Var}(D|X=x)P(X=x)}\)<br>Weights cells by treatment variance (most balanced cells get most weight).<br><br>If effects are heterogeneous AND the treated are concentrated in different cells than where treatment variance is highest, the estimates will differ.<br><br><b>When they're close (like here):</b> Either effects are fairly homogeneous, or the weighting differences don't matter much. You want to evaluate a training program. You have two pre-treatment years and one post-treatment year. You could use either matching or DiD. Under what circumstances would you prefer each?	<b>Prefer DiD when:</b><br>• Treatment and control groups have permanent level differences that you can't fully observe<br>• Selection into treatment is driven by time-invariant unobservables (ability, motivation as stable traits)<br>• Pre-trends are parallel (check with the two pre-periods)<br>• There might be unobserved confounders, but they're stable over time<br><br><b>Prefer matching when:</b><br>• Selection is driven by observable characteristics that you've comprehensively measured<br>• You have a credible story for why conditional on observables, treatment is quasi-random<br>• Common trends might fail (e.g., Ashenfelter's dip — treated group has a transitory shock before treatment)<br>• Cross-sectional variation is more informative than time variation<br><br><b>Key trade-off:</b> DiD allows permanent unobserved differences but requires common trends. Matching allows different trends but requires no unobserved confounders. They fail in complementary ways.<br><br><b>Best of both worlds:</b> Combine — match on observables first, then do DiD on the matched sample.	CI_Scenarios::Matching_vs_DiD
You estimate Oster's bias-adjusted treatment effect using \(\hat{\beta}^S = 0.15\), \(\hat{\beta}^L = 0.12\), \(R^S = 0.05\), \(R^L = 0.25\), \(R_{max} = 0.50\), \(\delta = 1\). What do you conclude?	Bias-adjusted estimate:<br>\(\hat{\beta}^L - (\hat{\beta}^S - \hat{\beta}^L)\frac{R_{max} - R^L}{R^L - R^S} = 0.12 - (0.15 - 0.12)\frac{0.50 - 0.25}{0.25 - 0.05}\)<br><br>\(= 0.12 - 0.03 \times \frac{0.25}{0.20} = 0.12 - 0.03 \times 1.25 = 0.12 - 0.0375 = 0.0825\)<br><br><b>Interpretation:</b> Adding controls moved the coefficient from 0.15 to 0.12 (small movement) and \(R^2\) from 0.05 to 0.25 (big movement). The scaling factor is 1.25 — unobservables would need to cause only 1.25× the bias that observables caused.<br><br>The adjusted estimate (0.0825) is still positive, which is encouraging. Under equal selection (\(\delta = 1\)), the effect survives the adjustment.<br><br><b>But:</b> If you raised \(\delta\) to about 1.6, the adjusted estimate would hit zero. So the result isn't rock-solid — unobservables need to be only ~60% more important than observables to explain it away.	CI_Scenarios::Matching
A state adopts a new education policy in 2020. You compare test scores in that state to a neighboring state before and after 2020. The treated state's scores rise by 8 points while the control state's rise by 3 points. You plot pre-treatment trends for a DiD and they look parallel for 5 years before treatment. Can you confidently assume parallel trends hold post-treatment?	<b>No — parallel pre-trends are necessary but not sufficient.</b><br><br>Parallel pre-trends support the design but don't prove the counterfactual would have continued in parallel. You estimate a DiD using log wages. Your colleague points out that if common trends holds in levels, it won't hold in logs. Why is this important?	<b>The common trends assumption is NOT invariant to nonlinear transformations.</b><br><br>Suppose treated workers earn \$30,000 and control workers earn \$60,000. A common additive trend of \$3,000/year means:<br>• Treated: +10% growth<br>• Control: +5% growth<br><br>Parallel in levels → NOT parallel in logs (and vice versa).<br><br><b>Implications:</b><br>1. The choice of functional form for the outcome has <b>identifying implications</b>, not just statistical convenience<br>2. If you believe the growth process is multiplicative (percentage changes are equalized), use logs<br>3. If you believe the growth process is additive (dollar changes are equalized), use levels<br>4. The economic context should guide this choice<br><br><b>Best practice:</b> Report both specifications. If results are qualitatively similar, that's reassuring. If they differ, discuss which functional form is more appropriate for the application.	CI_Scenarios::DiD
You're using a TWFE specification with staggered treatment across 50 states over 20 years. Treatment effects grow over time (the policy becomes more effective with longer exposure). A colleague warns you about TWFE bias. Explain the problem and the fix.	<b>Goodman-Bacon (2021) problem:</b> TWFE decomposes into pairwise 2×2 DiDs. Some use already-treated states as controls for newly-treated states.<br><br>If effects grow with exposure, already-treated states' outcomes are trending UP relative to their no-treatment counterfactual. Using them as "controls" makes newly-treated states look like they're improving LESS than controls → <b>downward bias</b> or even wrong sign.<br><br><b>How bad is it?</b> Depends on how much weight the "forbidden comparisons" get. Weight depends on group sizes and treatment timing. States treated in the middle of the panel get more weight.<br><br><b>Fix — Callaway-Sant'Anna:</b><br>1. Compare: RCT identifies ATE. DiD identifies ATT. Why the difference?	<b>RCT with perfect compliance:</b> Randomization makes the treated group representative of the ENTIRE population. \((Y_1,Y_0) \perp D\), so the effect on the treated = effect on anyone = ATE.<br><br><b>DiD:</b> Treatment isn't randomized — specific units get treated based on their characteristics. The control group provides the counterfactual TREND for the treated group, but doesn't represent what the treated group would look like in levels.<br><br>The DiD estimates:<br>\(E[\Delta Y|D=1] - E[\Delta Y|D=0] = E[\beta_i|D=1] = \text{ATT}\)<br><br>This is the effect <b>for the people who were actually treated</b>.
